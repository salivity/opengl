Simulating PBR Microfacets in OpenGL

Physically Based Rendering (PBR) simulates microscopic surface irregularities by treating rough surfaces as collections of tiny, perfectly reflective mirrors called microfacets. Rather than modeling these microscopic structures geometrically, the OpenGL rendering pipeline handles them statistically within fragment shaders using the Cook-Torrance Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF). This approach mathematically estimates light scattering, shadowing, and reflectance based on material properties like roughness and metalness, transforming standard rasterized triangles into photorealistic surfaces.

The Microfacet Theory and the Cook-Torrance BRDF

At the microscopic level, most surfaces are not smooth. Microfacet theory assumes that a surface is composed of many microscopic planar facets, each reflecting light as a perfect specular mirror according to its individual normal vector (\(m\)). Only the microfacets whose normals align exactly with the halfway vector (\(H\)) between the view direction (\(V\)) and the light direction (\(L\)) reflect visible light toward the camera.

In an OpenGL pipeline, this behavior is evaluated per fragment using the specular component of the Cook-Torrance BRDF:

\[f_{spec} = \frac{D \cdot G \cdot F}{4 (N \cdot V)(N \cdot L)}\]

Where \(N\) is the surface normal, and the three primary functions—\(D\), \(G\), and \(F\)—represent the statistical distribution and behavior of the microfacets.

The Three Core Components

  1. Normal Distribution Function (\(D\)): The NDF calculates the concentration of microfacets aligned with the halfway vector \(H\). The industry-standard formulation is the Trowbridge-Reitz GGX distribution. In GLSL, this function takes surface roughness as an input; lower roughness values produce a tight, intense specular highlight, while higher values spread the microfacet orientations widely across the surface, resulting in a duller, broader reflection.

  2. Geometric Shadowing Function (\(G\)): Because microfacets have height variations, they can occlude one another. The geometry function models microfacet shadowing (where microfacets block incoming light) and masking (where microfacets block light reflected toward the viewer). OpenGL implementations commonly use the Smith method combined with the Schlick-GGX approximation, taking both view and light directions into account to darken grazing angles realistically.

  3. Fresnel Equation (\(F\)): The Fresnel function dictates the fraction of light reflected versus refracted at different viewing angles. Using the Fresnel-Schlick approximation, the shader computes reflectance based on the surface’s base reflectivity (\(F_0\)) and the angle between the view vector and the halfway vector. Surfaces become almost completely reflective mirrors at grazing angles (\(90^\circ\)).

Execution in the OpenGL Pipeline

The simulation of microfacets occurs during the rasterization and fragment processing stages:

  • Vertex Shader: Transforms vertex positions, normals, and tangent-space vectors into world space. These values are passed via interface blocks to the rasterizer, where they are interpolated across each primitive.
  • Fragment Shader: Receives the interpolated data and samples the material textures (albedo, roughness, metallic, and normal maps). A Tangent-Bitangent-Normal (TBN) matrix converts perturbed normals from the normal map into world space.
  • Vector Computation: The shader normalizes the surface normal (\(N\)), calculates the direction to the camera (\(V\)) and light source (\(L\)), and computes the halfway vector:
vec3 H = normalize(V + L);
  • BRDF Evaluation: The shader evaluates the \(D\), \(G\), and \(F\) functions using standard GLSL operations (dot, pow, clamp). The specular result is combined with the Lambertian diffuse reflection according to energy conservation: the light that is not reflected by microfacets is refracted into the material to produce diffuse color, attenuated by the metalness factor.
  • Output: The resulting linear color is accumulated across all active light sources, processed through HDR tonemapping, corrected for gamma, and written to the framebuffer.
