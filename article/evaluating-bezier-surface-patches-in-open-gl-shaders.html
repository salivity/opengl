Evaluating Bezier Surface Patches in OpenGL Shaders

This article explores the fundamentals of Bézier surface patches and explains how modern OpenGL utilizes GPU tessellation shaders to evaluate them dynamically in real time. Readers will learn the mathematical foundation behind bicubic parametric surfaces, how to bypass CPU-bound geometry bottlenecks, and the implementation details of computing smooth curved surfaces and analytical normals directly on the GPU.

What Are Bézier Surface Patches?

A Bézier surface patch is a parametric 3D surface defined by an array of control points. While a standard Bézier curve uses a single parameter \(u \in [0, 1]\) to interpolate points in space, a Bézier surface extends this concept into two dimensions using two parameters, \((u, v) \in [0, 1] \times [0, 1]\).

The most common variant in computer graphics is the bicubic Bézier patch, which is defined by a \(4 \times 4\) grid of 16 control points. The spatial position \(P(u, v)\) on the patch is computed as the tensor product of Bernstein basis polynomials:

\[P(u, v) = \sum_{i=0}^{3} \sum_{j=0}^{3} B_{i,3}(u) B_{j,3}(v) P_{i,j}\]

Where \(P_{i,j}\) represents the control point at row \(i\) and column \(j\), and \(B_{i,3}(t)\) represents the cubic Bernstein polynomials:

  • \(B_{0,3}(t) = (1 - t)^3\)
  • \(B_{1,3}(t) = 3t(1 - t)^2\)
  • \(B_{2,3}(t) = 3t^2(1 - t)\)
  • \(B_{3,3}(t) = t^3\)

Control points do not generally lie on the surface itself (except for the four corner points); instead, they exert a directional pull, allowing artists and CAD engines to manipulate complex, organic shapes using a compact set of coordinates.

Dynamic Evaluation in OpenGL

Traditionally, evaluating these equations required pre-tessellating the patch on the CPU into a fixed mesh of triangles. Modern OpenGL offloads this process entirely to the hardware through the tessellation pipeline (introduced in OpenGL 4.0), comprising the Tessellation Control Shader (TCS), the Primitive Generator, and the Tessellation Evaluation Shader (TES).

1. Vertex Shader Setup

Control points are submitted to OpenGL using the GL_PATCHES primitive type:

glPatchParameteri(GL_PATCH_VERTICES, 16);
glDrawArrays(GL_PATCHES, 0, numVertices);

The Vertex Shader typically performs minimal work, passing the raw 3D control points straight through to the tessellation stages without model-view projection.

2. Tessellation Control Shader (TCS)

The TCS determines the degree of subdivision dynamically. It runs once per control point of the output patch and sets the inner and outer tessellation levels (gl_TessLevelInner and gl_TessLevelOuter):

  • Dynamic Level of Detail (LOD): Tessellation levels can be scaled according to the patch’s distance from the camera or its screen-space footprint. Patches close to the camera receive high subdivision factors, while distant patches use lower factors.
  • Data Pass-Through: The TCS forwards the 16 control points to the evaluation stage via output patch variables.

3. Fixed-Function Primitive Generator

The hardware primitive generator takes the tessellation factors defined in the TCS and generates a regular grid of barycentric coordinates \((u, v)\) across the unit square domain \([0, 1] \times [0, 1]\).

4. Tessellation Evaluation Shader (TES)

The TES executes once for each vertex generated by the primitive generator. It receives the domain coordinate via the built-in variable gl_TessCoord.xy, representing \((u, v)\).

The TES performs the following operations:

  1. Evaluate Basis Functions: Calculate the Bernstein polynomials for \(u\) and \(v\) using gl_TessCoord.x and gl_TessCoord.y.
  2. Compute Position: Sum the weighted contributions of all 16 control points to determine the final local vertex position.
  3. Compute Analytical Normals: Exact surface normals can be derived analytically without neighboring vertex approximations. By taking the partial derivatives of the surface equation with respect to \(u\) and \(v\) (\(\frac{\partial P}{\partial u}\) and \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial v}\)), the shader computes two tangent vectors. The cross product of these vectors yields the true normal: \[\mathbf{N} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial P}{\partial v}\]
  4. Transform to Clip Space: Multiply the final evaluated position by the Model-View-Projection (MVP) matrix and output to gl_Position.

Advantages of GPU-Side Evaluation

Evaluating Bézier patches directly on the GPU offers significant performance and visual fidelity benefits:

  • Bandwidth Efficiency: Transmitting 16 control points over the PCIe bus replaces the need to send thousands of static vertices and index buffers.
  • Adaptive Smoothness: Silhouettes remain perfectly smooth at arbitrary zoom levels without popping artifacts, as tessellation factors adjust per frame.
  • Memory Savings: Storage requirements in GPU VRAM are drastically reduced compared to storing high-density triangle meshes.
