Perspective-Correct Texture Interpolation in OpenGL

This article explains the mathematical mechanics behind perspective-correct texture coordinate interpolation in OpenGL. In 3D computer graphics, applying standard linear interpolation to texture coordinates in 2D screen space causes visual distortion because perspective projection is non-linear. To maintain geometric accuracy, modern graphics pipelines like OpenGL project texture coordinates into homogeneous space, linearly interpolate the divided attributes along with the reciprocal of the clip-space \(w\) coordinate, and reconstruct the true coordinate per fragment through a final division.

The Problem with Affine Mapping

When a 3D primitive is projected onto a 2D screen, the projection transformation divides camera-space coordinates by their depth component, \(Z\). If texture coordinates \((u, v)\) are linearly interpolated directly across screen-space pixels using standard affine mapping:

\[u(t) = (1 - t)u_0 + t u_1 \quad \text{where } t \in [0, 1]\]

the texture appears warped and bent along polygon diagonals. This artifact occurs because equal step sizes across pixels on the 2D screen do not correspond to equal step sizes across the surface in 3D space due to perspective foreshortening.

The Linear Property of Homogeneous Coordinates

While an attribute \(A\) does not vary linearly with respect to screen-space coordinates \((x_s, y_s)\), the perspective projection geometry ensures that the ratio of that attribute to the homogeneous clip-space depth, \(\frac{A}{w}\), does vary linearly across screen space. Similarly, the reciprocal of the depth value, \(\frac{1}{w}\), is also linear in screen space.

In OpenGL, the clip-space coordinate produced by multiplying a vertex by the model-view-projection matrix is represented as:

\[\mathbf{v}_{\text{clip}} = \begin{bmatrix} x_c \\ y_c \\ z_c \\ w_c \end{bmatrix}\]

In a standard perspective projection matrix, \(w_c\) is typically proportional to \(-Z_{\text{eye}}\) (the distance from the camera).

Mathematical Derivation of Corrected Values

Let two vertices on the screen have screen-space coordinates \(\mathbf{p}_0\) and \(\mathbf{p}_1\) with corresponding attributes \(u_0, u_1\) and clip-space weights \(w_0, w_1\).

For any screen-space fragment located at an interpolation factor \(s \in [0, 1]\) between \(\mathbf{p}_0\) and \(\mathbf{p}_1\):

  1. Interpolate the depth reciprocal: \[\left(\frac{1}{w}\right)_s = (1 - s) \frac{1}{w_0} + s \frac{1}{w_1}\]

  2. Interpolate the projected texture coordinate: \[\left(\frac{u}{w}\right)_s = (1 - s) \frac{u_0}{w_0} + s \frac{u_1}{w_1}\]

  3. Recover the perspective-correct coordinate: Divide the interpolated coordinate-depth ratio by the interpolated depth reciprocal to eliminate the homogeneous factor: \[u_s = \frac{\left(\frac{u}{w}\right)_s}{\left(\frac{1}{w}\right)_s} = \frac{(1 - s) \frac{u_0}{w_0} + s \frac{u_1}{w_1}}{(1 - s) \frac{1}{w_0} + s \frac{1}{w_1}}\]

Triangle Interpolation via Barycentric Coordinates

During rasterization, OpenGL evaluates pixels using 2D screen-space barycentric coordinates \((\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \lambda_2)\) such that \(\lambda_0 + \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 1\). The formulation extends to triangles as follows:

First, the hardware rasterizer computes the interpolated attribute ratio and reciprocal \(w\):

\[\left(\frac{u}{w}\right)_{\text{pixel}} = \sum_{i=0}^{2} \lambda_i \frac{u_i}{w_i}\]

\[\left(\frac{1}{w}\right)_{\text{pixel}} = \sum_{i=0}^{2} \lambda_i \frac{1}{w_i}\]

Finally, before passing the varying attribute to the fragment shader, the GPU computes the quotient:

\[u_{\text{pixel}} = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^{2} \lambda_i \frac{u_i}{w_i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{2} \lambda_i \frac{1}{w_i}}\]

This operation guarantees that attributes maintain uniform spacing on the original 3D primitive regardless of viewing angle or distance from the camera. In modern OpenGL (GLSL), this computation is performed automatically for variables declared with the default smooth interpolation qualifier.
