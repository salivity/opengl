Frustum Partitioning for Cascaded Shadow Maps in OpenGL

Cascaded Shadow Mapping (CSM) resolves perspective aliasing over large viewing distances by dividing the camera view frustum into distinct sub-frusta along the view axis and rendering a dedicated shadow map for each segment. In modern OpenGL graphics pipelines, optimizing how this frustum is partitioned directly determines shadow clarity and depth texture utilization. This article covers the primary mathematical formulas for computing split distances—including linear, logarithmic, and hybrid schemes—along with geometric fitting and projection stabilization techniques necessary for artifact-free rendering.

Frustum Split Distribution Schemes

The core challenge of partitioning is deciding where each slice begins and ends along the z-axis between the near clipping plane (\(z_{near}\)) and the far clipping plane (\(z_{far}\)).

  • Linear Partitioning: Splits the frustum into evenly spaced intervals: \[z_i = z_{near} + (z_{far} - z_{near}) \times \frac{i}{m}\] where \(m\) is the total cascade count and \(i\) is the current split index. While mathematically simple, linear distribution allocates excessive depth resolution to distant regions while starving areas close to the camera, leading to visible pixelation in the foreground.

  • Logarithmic Partitioning: Distributes splits exponentially to mirror the non-linear distribution of perspective projection: \[z_i = z_{near} \times \left(\frac{z_{far}}{z_{near}}\right)^{\frac{i}{m}}\] This scheme provides dense resolution near the viewer where detail matters most. However, for large view distances with a small \(z_{near}\), it can over-concentrate splits extremely close to the camera, rendering subsequent splits too wide.

  • The Practical Split Scheme (PSSM): Combines linear and logarithmic distributions using an interpolation factor \(\lambda\) (typically between 0.5 and 0.9): \[z_i = \lambda \cdot z_{near} \left(\frac{z_{far}}{z_{near}}\right)^{\frac{i}{m}} + (1 - \lambda) \left(z_{near} + (z_{far} - z_{near}) \frac{i}{m}\right)\] This hybrid formula is the industry standard for OpenGL implementations because it balances high foreground fidelity with stable mid-to-far range coverage.

Bounding Volume Computation in Light Space

Once split distances are calculated, an orthographic projection matrix must be constructed for each cascade:

  1. Frustum Corner Extraction: For each split segment, compute the eight world-space corners of the sub-frustum. This is typically done by taking the inverse view-projection matrix of the split frustum and transforming the canonical view volume corners (\([-1, 1]\) in Normalized Device Coordinates) into world space.
  2. Light-Space Transformation: Transform the eight world-space corners into the directional light's coordinate space using the light’s view matrix.
  3. Axis-Aligned Bounding Box (AABB) Fitting: Calculate the minimum and maximum \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) extents of the transformed corners. These bounds form the orthographic projection parameters (glOrtho equivalent: left, right, bottom, top, near, far).

To prevent shadow map clipping when occluders exist outside the visible camera frustum, the light's near plane must be extended backward along the light direction vector to encompass potential shadow casters.

Projection Stabilization and Texel Snapping

A common artifact in cascaded shadow mapping is shadow edge shimmering (or crawling) during camera rotation or translation. Two techniques manage this behavior:

  • Bounding Spheres for Rotation Invariance: Instead of deriving a tight AABB directly from frustum corners—which causes the box dimensions to change whenever the camera rotates—fit a bounding sphere around each sub-frustum. The sphere's center and radius remain constant under camera rotation, maintaining a consistent orthographic projection size.
  • Texel Snapping for Translation Invariance: As the camera moves, the light-space bounding box slides continuously, causing sub-texel movement of shadow edges. To eliminate this, round the projection matrix's origin to multiples of the shadow map's world-space texel size: \[\text{WorldUnitsPerTexel} = \frac{\text{CascadeWidth}}{\text{ShadowMapResolution}}\] \[\text{SnappedOrigin} = \text{floor}\left(\frac{\text{Origin}}{\text{WorldUnitsPerTexel}}\right) \times \text{WorldUnitsPerTexel}\] Applying this offset to the light projection matrix locks shadow texels to a static world-space grid, ensuring completely stable shadow boundaries in OpenGL.
