NV Conservative Raster Optimizations in OpenGL

This article examines the hardware rasterization optimizations introduced by the NV_conservative_raster OpenGL extension and its subsequent revisions. It covers how conservative rasterization replaces costly software and geometry shader workarounds with dedicated GPU fixed-function logic, the operational modes available—such as overestimation, underestimation, dilation, and pre-snap evaluation—and how these mechanisms optimize real-time workloads like voxelization and occlusion culling.

Dedicated Fixed-Function Coverage Testing

Standard hardware rasterization only generates a fragment if a geometric primitive covers a pixel's center point or a designated multisample location. Prior to conservative rasterization, applications requiring guaranteed coverage for any pixel touching a triangle (such as sparse voxel octree construction or collision detection) had to expand triangle perimeters manually in a geometry shader.

The NV_conservative_raster extension offloads this entire process to the GPU’s fixed-function rasterizer. The hardware evaluates the polygon edges against the full bounding box of the pixel rather than point samples. By moving this test to dedicated hardware, the GPU eliminates the massive vertex and geometry shader amplification overhead, memory bandwidth bottlenecks, and redundant fragment generation associated with geometric dilation.

Sub-Pixel Pre-Snap Conservative Rasterization

Sub-pixel snapping can introduce cracks or unintended coverage drops when vertices snap to fixed-point grid locations before rasterization. The extension and its companion specifications introduce pre-snap triangle evaluation.

Hardware evaluates the exact floating-point vertices of a triangle against pixel boundaries before rounding them to the sub-pixel grid. This ensures mathematical conservatism: a fragment is guaranteed to be generated even if a thin or degenerate triangle would otherwise collapse or shift outside a pixel’s sample domain due to hardware sub-pixel precision limits.

Hardware Conservative Dilation

Through the related dilation control, the hardware rasterizer can apply a user-defined expansion offset (dilation) directly to triangle edges in screen space:

  • Zero-Cost Offset Application: The rasterizer automatically inflates the primitive by a floating-point pixel radius directly within the rasterization core.
  • Algorithmic Margin of Safety: Applications can dilate coverage by fractions of a pixel or multiple pixels to account for conservative filtering, blur kernels, or ray-tracing acceleration structure (BVH) generation without recalculating screen-space edge equations in shaders.

Conservative Underestimation

While default conservative rasterization generates a fragment if any portion of a pixel is touched (overestimation), the hardware also supports conservative underestimation. Under this mode:

  • A fragment is generated only if the primitive completely covers the entire pixel area.
  • Hardware logic tests all four corners of the pixel against the primitive's edges.
  • This provides a hardware-accelerated method for conservative occlusion culling, ensuring that a bounding volume completely occludes underlying geometry without false positives.

Throughput and Memory Improvements

By replacing compute- or geometry-driven workarounds with fixed-function evaluation, NV_conservative_raster optimizes rendering pipelines in three key areas:

  1. Primitive Setup Reduction: Eliminates primitive expansion in geometry shaders, keeping primitive setup pipelines running at peak fixed-function throughput.
  2. Bandwidth Conservation: Greatly reduces VRAM traffic by removing the need for auxiliary vertex buffers and intermediate geometry passes during voxelization.
  3. Culling Efficiency: Allows the early-Z and hierarchical-Z (HiZ) hardware to aggressively discard fragments that do not meet coverage thresholds before executing expensive fragment shader code.
