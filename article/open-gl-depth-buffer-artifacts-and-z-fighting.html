OpenGL Depth Buffer Artifacts and Z-Fighting

When points, fragments, or primitives share identical or nearly identical spatial coordinates along the depth axis in OpenGL, the rendering pipeline frequently produces visual artifacts such as flickering, stitching, or banding. Commonly referred to as "Z-fighting," this issue occurs because the graphics hardware cannot reliably determine which fragment lies closer to the viewer. This article examines the root causes of these artifacts along depth buffer boundaries—focusing on finite buffer precision, perspective projection non-linearity, and rasterization rounding errors—while outlining practical strategies to eliminate them.

Finite Depth Buffer Precision

The depth buffer (Z-buffer) stores distance values using a fixed number of bits, typically 16, 24, or 32 bits per fragment. Because digital representations have finite resolution, there is a limit to the smallest distinguishable difference between two depth values (machine epsilon). When two points overlap precisely or lie closer together than the minimum resolvable delta of the buffer, both fragments compute depth values that round to the exact same discrete integer or floating-point representation. During the depth test (GL_LESS or GL_LEQUAL), the GPU makes arbitrary determinations based on evaluation order, causing fragments from different surfaces to interweave unpredictably.

Non-Linear Depth Distribution in Perspective Projection

In standard perspective projection, the transformation from view space to normalized device coordinates (NDC) maps depth non-linearly using a reciprocal function (\(1/z\)). As a result:

  • The vast majority of precision is concentrated extremely close to the near clipping plane.
  • Depth precision degrades exponentially as the distance from the camera increases toward the far clipping plane.

When overlapping points are positioned even moderately far from the camera, the quantization steps between representable depth values become relatively large. Primitives that appear visually distinct in object space end up falling into the same discrete depth quantization bin, drastically increasing the likelihood of boundary artifacts.

Transformation and Interpolation Differences

Even when two points are defined with identical mathematical coordinates, they may arrive at slightly different final depth values due to differences in:

  1. Matrix Multiplication Order: Computing world, view, and projection matrices in different orders or across different shader passes introduces tiny floating-point discrepancies.
  2. Barycentric Interpolation: Vertices processed as part of different triangles or draw calls might be interpolated across fragments with differing floating-point tolerances, yielding slightly divergent interpolated \(z\)-values at the fragment stage.
  3. Compiler Optimizations: Shader compilers may rearrange arithmetic operations via fused multiply-add (FMA) instructions in one shader but not in another, breaking exact numerical equivalence.

Sub-Pixel Rasterization and Quantization

OpenGL rasterizes primitives by testing whether fragment centers or multi-sample locations fall within polygon boundaries. At exact shared boundaries, subtle floating-point rounding during screen-space coordinate evaluation can cause fragments to pass or fail depth and rasterization tests inconsistently. If depth tests use strict inequalities (GL_LESS), fragments that round to the exact value of an already rendered pixel are discarded, producing gaps or speckled boundaries.

Mitigating Depth Buffer Artifacts

To prevent artifacts caused by overlapping points along depth boundaries:

  • Adjust Clipping Planes: Bring the near clipping plane (\(z_{near}\)) as far forward as possible, and pull the far clipping plane (\(z_{far}\)) closer. Minimizing the ratio \(z_{far} / z_{near}\) distributes depth precision much more evenly across the scene.
  • Increase Buffer Precision: Use a 24-bit or 32-bit floating-point depth buffer (GL_DEPTH_COMPONENT24 or GL_DEPTH_COMPONENT32F) instead of a 16-bit buffer.
  • Apply Polygon Offset: Use glEnable(GL_POLYGON_OFFSET_FILL) and glPolygonOffset(factor, units) to artificially bias the computed depth values of one layer, cleanly separating coplanar geometry in the depth test.
  • Implement Reversed-Z: In modern pipelines, mapping the near plane to \(1.0\) and the far plane to \(0.0\) in conjunction with a floating-point depth buffer provides a nearly linear distribution of precision across the entire viewing frustum.
