Conservative Rasterization in OpenGL Voxelization

Conservative rasterization serves as a fundamental hardware feature in modern OpenGL that guarantees watertight, accurate 3D scene voxelization. By altering the traditional graphics pipeline to generate fragments whenever a primitive touches any part of a pixel—rather than requiring the primitive to cover a specific subpixel sample point—conservative rasterization eliminates holes, gaps, and structural artifacts in the resulting voxel grid. This article explores how standard rasterization fails during voxelization, how conservative rasterization resolves these limitations, and the critical role it plays in techniques like Voxel Cone Tracing and volumetric rendering.

The Limitation of Standard Rasterization

In standard OpenGL rasterization, a fragment is only generated if a polygon covers the designated sample point of a pixel, typically its center. While this approach produces clean, anti-aliased images on 2D viewports, it fails when transforming 3D geometric meshes into discrete 3D texture volumes (voxel grids).

Thin geometry, edges viewed at oblique angles, or primitives smaller than a single voxel cell often fail to intersect the central sample point. Consequently, the fragment shader is never invoked for those regions. When voxelizing a scene, these dropped fragments result in missing voxels, leading to structural cracks in what should be solid surfaces.

How Conservative Rasterization Solves the Problem

Conservative rasterization alters the rasterizer's evaluation criteria. Instead of testing whether a polygon encompasses the center of a pixel, it tests whether the polygon intersects the pixel's bounding area at all.

When enabled in OpenGL (typically via extensions such as GL_NV_conservative_raster or GL_INTEL_conservative_rasterization), the hardware inflates the primitive's screen-space footprint by half a pixel in all directions. If any part of a triangle touches a voxel's footprint, a fragment is dispatched. This ensures that every piece of geometry, no matter how thin or angled, is captured and processed by the fragment shader.

The Voxelization Pipeline in Modern OpenGL

During real-time voxelization, modern OpenGL avoids traditional render-to-framebuffer pipelines and instead writes directly to a 3D texture using random-access image load/store operations (imageStore in GLSL). The pipeline typically functions as follows:

  1. Dominant Axis Projection: A geometry shader evaluates the face normal of each triangle and swizzles its coordinates to project it along its dominant axis (X, Y, or Z). This maximizes the surface area projected onto the 2D rasterization plane.
  2. Conservative Fragment Generation: With GL_CONSERVATIVE_RASTERIZATION_NV enabled via glEnable, the rasterizer ensures that any voxel cell touched by the projected triangle produces a fragment invocation.
  3. 3D Grid Writing: The fragment shader unswizzles the coordinates back into 3D world space, calculates the corresponding voxel coordinates, and commits color, normal, and material data to a 3D texture using atomic operations or standard image writes.

Without hardware conservative rasterization, developers historically had to manually expand triangle edges within the geometry shader. This software approach introduces significant computational overhead, creates redundant fragment invocations, and suffers from precision errors at triangle seams. Hardware-based conservative rasterization performs this boundary dilation with minimal performance cost.

Downstream Implications

The primary benefit of conservative rasterization is topological integrity. Advanced global illumination algorithms, such as Voxel Global Illumination (VXGI) and Voxel Cone Tracing, rely on a continuous, gap-free representation of scene geometry.

If thin geometry—such as walls, doors, or foliage—contains missing voxels, cone-traced secondary rays will pass straight through solid barriers, resulting in severe light leaks and incorrect ambient occlusion. Additionally, physics engines and pathfinding systems that utilize voxel representations require strict volumetric continuity to prevent collision clipping and entity tunneling. Conservative rasterization provides the baseline guarantee that voxel data accurately reflects the physical boundaries of the underlying scene.
