OpenGL Legacy Lighting: Ambient, Diffuse, Specular

This article explores the mathematical mechanics behind the legacy OpenGL fixed-function lighting pipeline. Before the advent of programmable vertex and fragment shaders, OpenGL relied on a configurable implementation of the Blinn-Phong reflection model computed per vertex. Below is a detailed breakdown of how OpenGL combined ambient, diffuse, and specular components to evaluate the final perceived color of a surface.

The General Lighting Model

In the legacy fixed-function pipeline, the total color \(C\) of a vertex was evaluated as the sum of material emission, scene-level ambient light, and the individual contributions of enabled light sources. The standard formula per light source is:

\[Color = Emission + Ambient_{global} + \sum (Attenuation \times Spotlight \times (Ambient + Diffuse + Specular))\]

The lighting calculations operated entirely on directional vectors transformed into eye coordinates, using the surface normal, light direction, and viewer direction.

1. Ambient Component

The ambient component approximates indirect, scattered environmental light that illuminates an object uniformly from all directions, preventing unlit areas from appearing completely black.

OpenGL calculates ambient reflection in two parts: a global scene ambient term and an individual light-source ambient term. The formula for a specific light source is:

\[Ambient = M_a \times L_a\]

Where:

  • \(M_a\) is the material's ambient reflectance property (GL_AMBIENT).
  • \(L_a\) is the ambient color intensity of the light source (GL_AMBIENT).

This value is multiplied component-wise (RGBA). In addition to individual lights, OpenGL adds the global ambient contribution:

\[Ambient_{total} = (M_a \times S_a) + \sum (M_a \times L_a)\]

Where \(S_a\) is the global ambient light setting (GL_LIGHT_MODEL_AMBIENT). The ambient term is independent of surface orientation and light direction.

2. Diffuse Component

The diffuse component models Lambertian reflection, which accounts for light scattering uniformly across a matte surface. The brightness of the reflected light depends entirely on the angle at which the incoming light strikes the surface.

The diffuse reflection is computed using the dot product of the surface normal and the light direction:

\[Diffuse = \max(N \cdot L, 0) \times (M_d \times L_d)\]

Where:

  • \(N\) is the normalized unit normal vector of the surface.
  • \(L\) is the normalized unit vector pointing from the vertex toward the light source.
  • \(M_d\) is the material's diffuse reflectance (GL_DIFFUSE).
  • \(L_d\) is the light source's diffuse intensity (GL_DIFFUSE).

The dot product \(N \cdot L\) equals the cosine of the angle of incidence (\(\theta\)). If the surface faces directly toward the light, the dot product equals 1.0, generating maximum brightness. If the angle exceeds 90 degrees, the dot product drops below zero; the calculation clamps the result to zero to prevent surfaces pointing away from the light from being illuminated.

3. Specular Component

The specular component produces shiny highlights on glossy surfaces. While original Phong lighting uses the reflection vector, legacy OpenGL implements the Blinn-Phong formulation for performance reasons, calculating the angle between the surface normal and a halfway vector.

The halfway vector \(H\) represents the direction halfway between the light source and the viewer:

\[H = \frac{L + V}{\|L + V\|}\]

Where:

  • \(L\) is the unit vector to the light source.
  • \(V\) is the unit vector pointing toward the viewer (camera).

The specular contribution is then calculated as:

\[Specular = \max(N \cdot H, 0)^s \times (M_s \times L_s)\]

Where:

  • \(N\) is the normalized unit normal vector.
  • \(H\) is the normalized halfway vector.
  • \(s\) is the material's shininess exponent (GL_SHININESS), ranging from 0 to 128.
  • \(M_s\) is the material's specular reflectance (GL_SPECULAR).
  • \(L_s\) is the light source's specular intensity (GL_SPECULAR).

Raising \(N \cdot H\) to the power of the shininess exponent \(s\) concentrates the highlight into a smaller, sharper area, simulating smoother, more reflective surfaces. If the diffuse term \(N \cdot L \le 0\), OpenGL suppresses the specular term completely to ensure that back-facing surfaces do not receive highlights.

Attenuation and Interpolation

For positional light sources, the diffuse, specular, and light-specific ambient components are scaled by a distance attenuation factor:

\[Attenuation = \frac{1}{k_c + k_l d + k_q d^2}\]

Where \(d\) is the distance from the light to the vertex, and \(k_c\), \(k_l\), and \(k_q\) are the constant, linear, and quadratic attenuation coefficients defined via GL_CONSTANT_ATTENUATION, GL_LINEAR_ATTENUATION, and GL_QUADRATIC_ATTENUATION.

Because these mathematical evaluations occurred exclusively at the vertex level, OpenGL used Gouraud shading: the calculated colors were passed down the graphics pipeline and linearly interpolated across the fragments of the primitive, creating smooth transitions over the geometry.
