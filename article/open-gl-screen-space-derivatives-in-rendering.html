OpenGL Screen-Space Derivatives in Rendering

Screen-space derivatives in OpenGL—evaluated using GLSL functions such as dFdx, dFdy, and fwidth—calculate the rate of change of any variable across a 2x2 pixel quad. These built-in calculations are fundamental to modern real-time graphics pipelines, enabling shaders to deduce geometric, depth, and spatial variations without precomputed vertex attributes. By measuring hardware-level differences between adjacent fragments, several rendering techniques rely directly on these screen-space derivatives to handle texture sampling, antialiasing, lighting, and geometric reconstruction efficiently.

Automatic Texture Mipmapping and Filtering

The most widespread implicit use of screen-space derivatives is the selection of texture Level of Detail (LoD) and anisotropic filtering. When calling standard sampling functions like texture(), the GPU calculates the derivative of texture coordinates (\(u, v\)) with respect to screen coordinates (\(x, y\)). This footprint indicates how much texture space is covered by a single screen pixel. If the derivative is high, the surface is viewed at a distance or at an acute angle, triggering the hardware to sample higher mipmap levels to prevent undersampling and texture aliasing. Explicit derivatives via textureGrad() allow developers to supply custom derivatives when branching causes native derivative evaluation to become undefined.

Procedural Pattern Antialiasing

When generating procedural textures, such as stripes, checkerboards, or grid lines, sharp mathematical edges cause severe spatial aliasing. The fwidth function, which computes the sum of the absolute derivatives (abs(dFdx(v)) + abs(dFdy(v))), determines the rate of change across pixel boundaries. By combining fwidth with the smoothstep function, shaders create an analytical filter region that smoothly transitions across boundaries over a span of one to two pixels, producing clean, antialiased edges regardless of the camera's angle or distance.

Dynamic Normal Generation (Faceted Shading)

Screen-space derivatives allow fragment shaders to compute face normals on the fly without storing or interpolating vertex normals. By evaluating the cross product of the screen-space derivatives of the interpolated surface position:

vec3 normal = normalize(cross(dFdx(v_position), dFdy(v_position)));

the shader dynamically determines the geometric normal for the current polygon. This technique is standard for rendering low-poly art styles, dynamic tessellation results, or complex geometry formats where per-vertex normals are omitted to conserve memory.

Tangent-Free Normal and Bump Mapping

Traditional tangent-space normal mapping requires precomputed per-vertex tangent and bitangent vectors. Screen-space derivatives eliminate this requirement by computing the surface gradient directly in the fragment shader. By calculating the partial derivatives of both the world-space surface position and the texture coordinates, a shader can reconstruct the local tangent frame on demand. This approach avoids tangent attribute storage, handles non-uniform UV scaling correctly, and resolves mirrored UV seam artifacts seamlessly.

Normal Reconstruction from Depth

Deferred rendering and post-processing passes (such as Screen-Space Ambient Occlusion, or SSAO) often require view-space or world-space normals. Instead of allocating memory to store high-precision normal G-buffers, engines can reconstruct normals dynamically from a single depth buffer. By using dFdx and dFdy on the reconstructed view-space positions derived from linear depth, the fragment shader computes accurate surface orientations directly during post-processing passes.
