Anisotropic Filtering in OpenGL Explained

This article explores how anisotropic filtering (AF) eliminates texture blurring on surfaces viewed at oblique angles in OpenGL applications. It covers the geometric limitations of traditional isotropic mipmapping, details how anisotropic algorithms sample elliptical footprints in texture space, and explains how to configure anisotropic filtering parameters within the OpenGL pipeline.

The Limits of Isotropic Mipmapping

Standard texturing techniques in OpenGL use isotropic mipmapping, such as bilinear or trilinear filtering. Isotropic filtering assumes that a square screen-space pixel maps to a roughly square area—or footprint—in texture space (UV space). This assumption holds true when a surface is facing the camera directly.

When a textured polygon recedes into the distance at a steep angle, such as a floor or a runway, perspective compression occurs along one axis. As a result, a square pixel projects onto the surface as an elongated, stretched ellipse or trapezoid.

Because standard mipmapping only uses square texel regions, the GPU must choose how to sample this elongated footprint:

  • If it chooses a mipmap level based on the shorter axis (undersampling), the texture shimmers and aliases along the stretched axis.
  • If it chooses a mipmap level based on the longer axis (oversampling to prevent aliasing), the texture is heavily downsampled along both axes.

Standard trilinear filtering chooses the latter approach to eliminate aliasing, which causes severe, muddy blurring on surfaces viewed at grazing angles.

How Anisotropic Filtering Solves Blurring

Anisotropic filtering resolves this distortion by decoupling the horizontal and vertical sampling rates based on the surface angle. The algorithm works through several key steps:

  1. Gradient Calculation: Using screen-space partial derivatives (dFdx and dFdy), the GPU analyzes how rapidly texture coordinates change relative to screen coordinates.
  2. Determining the Anisotropic Footprint: The GPU determines the degree of anisotropy—the ratio between the major (longest) and minor (shortest) axes of the projected pixel footprint—and identifies the direction in which the distortion occurs.
  3. Selecting a Higher-Detail Mipmap: Instead of dropping down to a low-resolution mipmap to cover the major axis, AF selects a mipmap level that corresponds to the higher resolution of the minor axis.
  4. Multiple Sampling (Ripmapping/Slicing): Along the line of anisotropy (the major axis), the GPU takes multiple anisotropic texture samples (sub-samples) from the chosen mipmap level.
  5. Weighted Blending: The GPU averages these multiple samples together to produce the final fragment color.

By sampling multiple points along the trajectory of perspective compression, anisotropic filtering preserves sharp texture details across the non-compressed axis while simultaneously preventing aliasing along the compressed axis.

Implementation in OpenGL

In OpenGL, anisotropic filtering is supported via the GL_ARB_texture_filter_anisotropic extension or as standard core functionality in OpenGL 4.6.

To implement anisotropic filtering, applications first query the maximum supported anisotropy level provided by the graphics driver:

GLfloat maxAnisotropy = 0.0f;
glGetFloatv(GL_MAX_TEXTURE_MAX_ANISOTROPY, &maxAnisotropy);

The application then applies this filter to a bound texture object using glTexParameterf:

glBindTexture(GL_TEXTURE_2D, textureID);
glTexParameterf(GL_TEXTURE_2D, GL_TEXTURE_MIN_FILTER, GL_LINEAR_MIPMAP_LINEAR);
glTexParameterf(GL_TEXTURE_2D, GL_TEXTURE_MAX_ANISOTROPY, maxAnisotropy);

A value of 1.0f disables anisotropic filtering (defaulting to standard isotropic filtering), while values such as 4.0f, 8.0f, or 16.0f scale the maximum ratio of texel samples the hardware will gather along the stretched axis. Higher values produce clearer textures at extreme viewing angles with minimal impact on modern graphics hardware performance.
