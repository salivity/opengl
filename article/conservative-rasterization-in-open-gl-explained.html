Conservative Rasterization in OpenGL Explained

This article explores conservative rasterization, a specialized GPU rendering technique that alters the standard rasterization pipeline to ensure every pixel touched by a geometric primitive generates a fragment. It covers how conservative rasterization differs from standard sample-point rasterization, its primary use cases in computer graphics—such as voxelization and collision detection—and the specific OpenGL extensions used to enable and configure this feature across different hardware vendors.

Understanding Standard vs. Conservative Rasterization

In the standard OpenGL rasterization pipeline, a primitive (such as a triangle) produces a fragment only if its area covers a specific sample point within the pixel grid—typically the exact center of the pixel. If a triangle slices through a corner or edge of a pixel without covering this central sample point, the pixel is ignored. While this approach prevents pixel double-counting in standard rasterization, it creates gaps when complete geometric coverage is required.

Conservative rasterization alters this rule. Instead of testing discrete sample points, it tests the entire area of the pixel. If any part of a primitive touches or intersects any portion of the pixel footprint, the GPU generates a fragment.

Hardware generally implements this through two variations:

  • Overestimated Conservative Rasterization: Generates a fragment if the primitive touches any part of the pixel area. This is the default and most widely used mode.
  • Underestimated Conservative Rasterization: Generates a fragment only if the primitive completely encloses the entire pixel area.

Common Use Cases

Conservative rasterization is critical for rendering and computational algorithms that cannot tolerate false negatives:

  • GPU Voxelization: Converting 3D surface meshes into regular 3D grid volumes (voxels) without missing thin geometry or surface cracks.
  • Occlusion Culling: Safely determining whether objects are hidden without risking false culling of visible geometry.
  • Collision Detection: Ensuring that geometric contact is never missed due to sampling blind spots.
  • Shadow Mapping: Eliminating light-leaking artifacts in techniques like deep shadow maps or cascaded shadow maps.

OpenGL Extensions for Conservative Rasterization

OpenGL does not include conservative rasterization as part of its core specification; instead, it is exposed through vendor-specific extensions.

1. NVIDIA: GL_NV_conservative_raster

NVIDIA introduced the primary framework for conservative rasterization in OpenGL. The GL_NV_conservative_raster extension enables overestimated conservative rasterization.

To use it, applications verify support and enable the state:

glEnable(GL_CONSERVATIVE_RASTERIZATION_NV);

This extension also introduces functions to manage subpixel precision:

  • glSubpixelPrecisionBiasNV(GLuint xbits, GLuint ybits): Sets a bias to adjust the precision snapping to accommodate conservative bounds across hardware implementations.

NVIDIA also provides complementary extensions:

  • GL_NV_conservative_raster_dilate: Extends the conservative rasterizer to artificially dilate polygons in screen space using glConservativeRasterDilateNV().
  • GL_NV_conservative_raster_pre_snap_triangles: Ensures that triangles are evaluated conservatively before vertex snapping occurs, avoiding geometric inaccuracies.
  • GL_NV_conservative_raster_underestimation: Exposes the underestimated rasterization mode.

2. Intel: GL_INTEL_conservative_rasterization

Intel provides conservative rasterization on supported Gen hardware via the GL_INTEL_conservative_rasterization extension.

Similar to the NVIDIA extension, it is toggled as an OpenGL state:

glEnable(GL_CONSERVATIVE_RASTERIZATION_INTEL);

When enabled, any pixel whose square area is touched by a triangle generates a fragment. This extension operates similarly to the overestimated mode of the NVIDIA counterpart.

Practical Implementation Considerations

  • Depth Values: In conservative rasterization, fragments generated along the outer edges of a triangle lie outside the triangle's actual mathematical boundary. By default, extrapolated depth values may exceed standard [0, 1] ranges or cause unexpected clipping, requiring careful handling or clamping via depth bounds.
  • Fragment Shader Invocations: Conservative rasterization significantly increases fragment shader invocations because boundary pixels that would normally be skipped are processed. To maintain real-time performance, it is often paired with lightweight fragment shaders or empty fragment shaders using image load/store operations.
