Point Light Attenuation Factors in OpenGL

In real-time computer graphics with OpenGL, calculating realistic point light attenuation relies on a quadratic formula to simulate how light diminishes over distance. This article breaks down the standard three-term attenuation equation—constant, linear, and quadratic factors—explaining how each parameter influences illumination, why the inverse-square law dictates real-world physics, and how to calibrate these factors for both physical plausibility and visual control in shaders.

The Attenuation Formula

In modern OpenGL fragment shaders and legacy fixed-function pipelines, the intensity of light received by a fragment from a point light source is scaled by an attenuation factor. The standard equation is:

\[\text{Attenuation} = \frac{1.0}{K_c + K_l \cdot d + K_q \cdot d^2}\]

In this formula, \(d\) represents the Euclidean distance between the light source position and the surface fragment being shaded. The denominator consists of three customizable coefficients: \(K_c\), \(K_l\), and \(K_q\).

The Three Quadratic Factors

1. Constant Factor (\(K_c\))

The constant factor \(K_c\) is typically fixed at 1.0. Its primary purpose is to ensure that the denominator never evaluates to zero when the distance \(d\) approaches zero, which prevents division-by-zero artifacts. It also ensures that the maximum calculated attenuation factor remains 1.0 directly at the light position, meaning the light never amplifies surface brightness beyond its base intensity.

2. Linear Factor (\(K_l\))

The linear factor \(K_l\) scales proportionally with distance (\(d\)). When active, it reduces light intensity at a steady, linear rate across intermediate distances. While linear falloff is not strictly physically accurate, it gives artists control over softening the light transition and preventing the steep drop-off associated with purely quadratic calculations at medium ranges.

3. Quadratic Factor (\(K_q\))

The quadratic factor \(K_q\) scales with the square of the distance (\(d^2\)). This factor directly models the physical behavior of light in three dimensions. As photons travel outward from an omnidirectional point source, they distribute evenly across the surface of an expanding sphere (\(A = 4\pi r^2\)). Consequently, the energy density per unit area decreases proportionally to the square of the distance. The quadratic term causes rapid dimming near the source and a gradual flattening of light decay over long distances.

Achieving Physical Accuracy

In the real physical world, light falloff is strictly governed by the inverse-square law:

\[\text{Intensity} \propto \frac{1}{d^2}\]

To configure pure physical falloff within the standard attenuation framework:

  • Set \(K_c = 1.0\) (or use a small radius offset \(\epsilon\) to prevent the singularity at \(d = 0\)).
  • Set \(K_l = 0.0\).
  • Set \(K_q = 1.0\) (scaled to match the light's intended intensity radius).

In physically based rendering (PBR) pipelines, lighting calculations frequently drop the legacy linear factor entirely, using an attenuation model defined as:

\[\text{Attenuation} = \frac{1.0}{\max(d^2, \epsilon)}\]

Where \(\epsilon\) is a minimum distance threshold to avoid infinite intensity at the center.

Choosing Factor Values for Finite Ranges

Because a pure \(\frac{1}{d^2}\) function approaches zero asymptotically and never reaches absolute darkness, real-time renderers often combine \(K_c\), \(K_l\), and \(K_q\) to force the light to effectively reach near-zero visibility within a specific radius, avoiding expensive calculations for distant surfaces:

  • Short Range (Radius ~7): \(K_c = 1.0\), \(K_l = 0.7\), \(K_q = 1.8\)
  • Medium Range (Radius ~50): \(K_c = 1.0\), \(K_l = 0.09\), \(K_q = 0.032\)
  • Long Range (Radius ~3250): \(K_c = 1.0\), \(K_l = 0.0014\), \(K_q = 0.000007\)

Balancing \(K_q\) with \(K_l\) ensures smooth falloff while retaining the visual cues of real-world photometric dispersion.
