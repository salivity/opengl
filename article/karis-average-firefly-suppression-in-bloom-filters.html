Karis Average Firefly Suppression in Bloom Filters

Physically based bloom pipelines in modern OpenGL renderers rely on multi-pass downsampling and upsampling chains to produce smooth, photorealistic light bleeding from high dynamic range (HDR) light sources. However, extremely bright subpixel specular highlights often produce distracting temporal artifacts and swimming noise, commonly known as "fireflies." This article explains what the Karis average is, why fireflies emerge in physically based bloom filters, and how this luminance-weighted filtering technique effectively mitigates single-pixel energy spikes without muddying the overall lighting presentation.

The Firefly Problem in HDR Bloom

In a physically based lighting pipeline, HDR surfaces, emissive materials, and specular reflections can emit luminance values orders of magnitude higher than standard monitor capabilities (e.g., values exceeding 1,000.0 or 10,000.0 alongside standard mid-tones around 1.0).

When these values occupy only one or two pixels—typical of fine metallic edges or sharp roughness reflections—standard downsampling algorithms struggle. A naive box filter or standard bilinear downsample takes a mathematical mean of neighboring pixels. Because the intensity of an HDR firefly is disproportionately high, a single pixel can dominate an entire \(2 \times 2\) or \(4 \times 4\) downsampled quad. As the camera moves even slightly, this high-energy highlight enters and leaves different pixel footprints, causing the downsampled bloom mip chain to flicker, pop, and pulse violently between frames.

What is the Karis Average?

The Karis average is a partial tone-mapping, luminance-weighted downsampling approach introduced by Brian Karis at Epic Games. Rather than averaging neighboring samples linearly, the Karis average calculates a weighting factor for each sample based on its perceived brightness.

The formula typically relies on a rational function derived from simple Reinhard tone mapping:

\[w_i = \frac{1}{1 + L(c_i)}\]

Where:

  • \(c_i\) is the sampled HDR color vector (RGB).
  • \(L(c_i)\) is the scalar perceived luminance of that color, usually computed via standard luminance weights: \(\text{dot}(c_i, \text{vec3}(0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722))\).
  • \(w_i\) is the resulting scalar weight applied to sample \(c_i\).

The filtered output color \(C_{out}\) is then calculated as the weighted sum divided by the sum of all weights:

\[C_{out} = \frac{\sum_{i} (w_i \cdot c_i)}{\sum_{i} w_i}\]

How the Karis Average Suppresses Fireflies

The mechanism that suppresses fireflies is the dampening effect the weight \(w_i\) has on high-energy pixels:

  1. Energy Compression: For pixels with low or moderate luminance (\(L \approx 1.0\)), the weight remains close to \(0.5\), allowing neighboring non-extreme pixels to retain equal influence. For a firefly pixel with extreme luminance (\(L = 10,000.0\)), the weight drops to roughly \(0.0001\).
  2. Preventing Quad Takeover: In standard averaging, one pixel with a value of \(10,000.0\) surrounded by three pixels with values of \(1.0\) produces an average value of \(2,500.75\). With the Karis average, multiplying by the luminance weight effectively squashes the extreme pixel down to approximately \(1.0\) during the intermediate accumulation, preventing it from overwhelming the localized average.
  3. Temporal Stability: By dampening individual subpixel spikes before they spread across the mip chain, the localized energy level remains stable across consecutive frames, removing the jarring flickering effect without destroying the perceived bloom effect.

Implementation in an OpenGL Bloom Chain

Modern bloom techniques, such as the 13-tap tent filter popularized by Jorge Jimenez, construct a mip chain by downsampling the screen-space HDR buffer through multiple iterations.

Because the Karis average alters the linear radiance distribution, it should only be executed during the initial downsample pass (from the full-resolution HDR framebuffer to the first mip level). Applying the Karis average across every downsampling stage would progressively dim the entire bloom buffer and violate energy conservation principles. Once fireflies are suppressed in the first downsampling stage, subsequent downsampling and upsampling passes can safely use standard linear weighted filters.

In an OpenGL fragment shader for the first downsample pass, a 13-tap filter is typically divided into overlapping groups of four samples (quads). The Karis average is computed per quad to retain local contrast:

// Relative luminance helper
float Luminance(vec3 color) {
    return dot(color, vec3(0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722));
}

// Karis weight helper
float KarisWeight(vec3 color) {
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + Luminance(color));
}

// Example quad downsample using Karis averaging
vec3 DownsampleQuad(vec3 c0, vec3 c1, vec3 c2, vec3 c3) {
    float w0 = KarisWeight(c0);
    float w1 = KarisWeight(c1);
    float w2 = KarisWeight(c2);
    float w3 = KarisWeight(c3);

    return (c0 * w0 + c1 * w1 + c2 * w2 + c3 * w3) / (w0 + w1 + w2 + w3);
}

By computing and applying these weights during the initial downsample, the OpenGL bloom filter prevents extreme isolated luminance spikes from degrading visual fidelity while maintaining the broad, smooth glow expected from physically based rendering.
