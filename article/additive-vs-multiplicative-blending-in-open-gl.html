Additive vs Multiplicative Blending in OpenGL

This article examines the core differences between additive blending and multiplicative blending in OpenGL, detailing their mathematical formulations, implementation using glBlendFunc, and typical rendering use cases. Readers will learn how these two blending modes manipulate color buffers differently to achieve either light-emitting effects or light-absorbing effects in computer graphics.

The OpenGL Blending Equation

OpenGL determines the final pixel color written to the framebuffer using the blending equation:

\[\text{Final Color} = (\text{Source Color} \times \text{Source Factor}) + (\text{Destination Color} \times \text{Destination Factor})\]

The source color represents the incoming fragment, while the destination color represents the pixel currently residing in the framebuffer. The distinction between additive and multiplicative blending lies in how these factors are configured.

What is Additive Blending?

Additive blending adds the color values of the incoming fragment directly to the existing pixels in the framebuffer.

OpenGL Configuration

The standard configuration for pure additive blending is:

glBlendFunc(GL_ONE, GL_ONE);

Alternatively, to factor in the texture's alpha channel:

glBlendFunc(GL_SRC_ALPHA, GL_ONE);

Visual Characteristics and Math

  • Formula: \(\text{Final Color} = \text{Source Color} + \text{Destination Color}\)
  • Behavior: Colors accumulate toward pure white (1.0, 1.0, 1.0).
  • Neutral Color: Pure black (0.0, 0.0, 0.0) has no effect on the output. Adding black leaves the underlying pixel unchanged.
  • Overdraw Result: Stacking multiple additive layers increases the brightness of the scene.

Primary Use Cases

  • Fire, flames, and sparks
  • Energy shields and lasers
  • Light glows, lens flares, and blooms
  • Holograms and ethereal magic effects

What is Multiplicative Blending?

Multiplicative blending multiplies the color values of the incoming fragment by the values of the existing framebuffer pixels.

OpenGL Configuration

The standard configuration for multiplicative blending is:

glBlendFunc(GL_DST_COLOR, GL_ZERO);

Or equivalently:

glBlendFunc(GL_ZERO, GL_SRC_COLOR);

Visual Characteristics and Math

  • Formula: \(\text{Final Color} = \text{Source Color} \times \text{Destination Color}\)
  • Behavior: Colors scale downward toward pure black (0.0, 0.0, 0.0) because color channels are normalized between 0.0 and 1.0.
  • Neutral Color: Pure white (1.0, 1.0, 1.0) has no effect on the output, functioning as an identity element.
  • Overdraw Result: Stacking multiple multiplicative layers darkens the scene.

Primary Use Cases

  • Projected shadows and ambient occlusion
  • Lightmaps baked onto textures
  • Tinted or stained glass
  • Dirt, grime, and blood decals on surfaces

Key Technical Comparisons

Feature Additive Blending Multiplicative Blending
glBlendFunc (GL_ONE, GL_ONE) (GL_DST_COLOR, GL_ZERO)
Mathematical Operation Addition (\(S + D\)) Multiplication (\(S \times D\))
Luminance Effect Increases brightness Decreases brightness
Neutral Element Black (0.0) leaves surface unchanged White (1.0) leaves surface unchanged
Saturation Target Pure White (1.0, 1.0, 1.0) Pure Black (0.0, 0.0, 0.0)
Physical Analogy Light emission (combining light beams) Light absorption (filtering through stained glass)

In summary, additive blending simulates emitted light by combining color values toward white, making it ideal for glowing visual effects. Multiplicative blending simulates absorption by scaling values down toward black, making it ideal for shadows, filters, and surface shading.
