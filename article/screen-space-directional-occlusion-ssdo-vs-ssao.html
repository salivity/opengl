Screen Space Directional Occlusion: SSDO vs SSAO

Screen Space Directional Occlusion (SSDO) is an advanced real-time rendering technique designed to approximate indirect illumination and realistic contact shadows entirely in post-processing. While standard Screen Space Ambient Occlusion (SSAO) estimates uniform ambient shadow darkening in crevices, SSDO incorporates the directionality of incoming light to produce accurate directional shadows and subtle indirect color bleeding. This article explores the fundamentals of SSDO, compares its mechanics to traditional SSAO, and explains how it dramatically enhances visual fidelity in OpenGL rendering pipelines.

Understanding SSAO and Its Limitations

Screen Space Ambient Occlusion works by analyzing the depth buffer (and often surface normals) to determine how occluded a given point is by nearby geometry. If a fragment is surrounded by geometry that exceeds a certain depth threshold within a sampling sphere or hemisphere, SSAO scales down the ambient lighting at that point.

However, standard SSAO has critical physical inaccuracies:

  • Non-Directional Attenuation: SSAO assumes ambient light arrives uniformly from all directions. It applies a uniform scalar darkening factor regardless of whether a light source is directly illuminating the crevice.
  • Over-Darkening (Halos): Surfaces directly hit by bright directional lights can still display dark SSAO halos, breaking the realism of the scene.
  • No Color Bleeding: SSAO only computes a monochrome visibility factor (\(0.0\) to \(1.0\)) and cannot simulate the bouncing of colored light between adjacent surfaces.

How SSDO Works

Screen Space Directional Occlusion resolves these limitations by treating ambient illumination as directional rather than omnidirectional. Instead of calculating a single occlusion scalar, SSDO samples nearby depth and normal data to determine whether geometry blocks light originating from specific directions, typically evaluated against direct environment maps or directional light sources.

During computation, SSDO classifies sampled neighboring points into two categories:

  1. Occluders: Points that block direct light from reaching the current surface fragment.
  2. Inter-reflectors (Transmitters): Points that can bounce their own reflected light (albedo) onto the current surface fragment.

By differentiating these interactions, SSDO simultaneously calculates directional shadowing (blocking direct light) and one-bounce indirect diffuse illumination (color bleeding from neighboring geometry).

Key Improvements Over Standard SSAO

  • Direction-Aware Contact Shadows: In SSDO, an occluder only casts an ambient shadow if it lies directly in the path of the incoming light. If a crevice is exposed to the primary light direction, no artificial darkening occurs.
  • One-Bounce Color Bleeding: When a neighbor occludes incoming light, SSDO reads the color of that occluding surface from the screen-space albedo buffer. It then adds this color to the current fragment as secondary bounced radiance, mimicking true global illumination.
  • Better Integration with Direct Lighting: Because SSDO outputs direct light occlusion vectors alongside diffuse color transfer, it blends naturally with directional lights and image-based lighting (IBL) without creating flat, unnatural ambient patches.

Implementing SSDO in OpenGL

Implementing SSDO in an OpenGL deferred or forward-plus rendering pipeline typically follows these steps:

  1. G-Buffer Generation: Render the scene geometry to multiple render targets (FBOs) to store view-space positions (or linear depth), view-space normals, and diffuse albedo.
  2. Sampling Kernel Execution: In a full-screen quad fragment shader or compute shader, sample points in an oriented hemisphere aligned with the surface normal.
  3. Occlusion and Radiance Evaluation:
    • Project each sample point into screen space and compare its depth with the G-Buffer depth.
    • If a sample is occluded, determine whether it blocks light coming from the light sources or environment.
    • If occluded, read the sample's stored albedo to compute the indirect bounced radiance toward the current fragment.
  4. Spatial Filtering: Like SSAO, SSDO uses randomized sample orientations to maintain low sample counts (usually 16–32 samples). This introduces high-frequency noise that must be smoothed using a geometry-aware bilateral blur pass, which respects depth and normal edges to prevent blurring across geometric boundaries.
  5. Lighting Composition: The filtered directional occlusion and bounced radiance are factored into the final lighting accumulation pass, attenuating ambient terms accurately and adding the indirect color bleed to the final frame.
