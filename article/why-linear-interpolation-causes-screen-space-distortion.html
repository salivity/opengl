Why Linear Interpolation Causes Screen Space Distortion

In 3D computer graphics, directly interpolating vertex attributes linearly across screen pixels produces severe visual distortion, such as warped textures and bent geometry. This occurs because the perspective projection that maps 3D world coordinates onto a 2D display is non-linear with respect to depth. To preserve correct spatial relationships, modern graphics pipelines like OpenGL perform perspective division by the homogeneous coordinate \(w\), transforming coordinates into normalized device coordinates and enabling perspective-correct interpolation.

The Mathematics of Perspective Projection

To project a 3D scene onto a 2D monitor, a camera frustum must be mapped into clip space via a projection matrix. In a real-world perspective view, objects farther away appear smaller. Mathematically, the projected position on the screen is inversely proportional to the camera-space depth (\(z\)).

When a 4D homogeneous clip-space coordinate \((x_c, y_c, z_c, w_c)\) is produced by the vertex shader, \(w_c\) typically holds the negated camera-space depth (\(-z_e\)). To transform this into Normalized Device Coordinates (NDC), OpenGL performs perspective division:

\[x_{ndc} = \frac{x_c}{w_c}, \quad y_{ndc} = \frac{y_c}{w_c}, \quad z_{ndc} = \frac{z_c}{w_c}\]

Because the divisor \(w_c\) varies across the surface of any primitive that is tilted relative to the camera, the mapping from 3D camera space to 2D screen space is non-linear.

Why Screen-Space Linear Interpolation Fails

Rasterization converts primitives into individual fragments on a 2D grid. If the hardware performs standard linear (affine) interpolation across screen space, it assumes uniform spacing. For an attribute \(A\) (such as a texture coordinate \(U\) or \(V\)) between two vertices, standard screen-space linear interpolation computes values as:

\[A(t) = (1 - t)A_0 + tA_1 \quad \text{where } t \in [0, 1]\]

While equal increments of \(t\) cover equal distances on the 2D screen, they do not correspond to equal distances in 3D world space. Because of foreshortening, a pixel near the far end of a receding polygon represents a much larger area of world space than a pixel near the front end.

Interpolating linearly across 2D pixels treats depth as constant. On textured surfaces, this results in "texture swimming" or unnatural warping along the shared diagonal edge of a quad, as textures stretch unevenly depending on camera angle.

The Mechanism of Perspective-Correct Interpolation

To solve this distortion, attribute interpolation must account for the non-linear relationship introduced by perspective projection. While the attribute \(A\) does not vary linearly in screen space, the quantity \(\frac{A}{w}\) and the reciprocal of depth \(\frac{1}{w}\) do vary linearly across screen space.

Rasterizers achieve perspective-correct interpolation through a three-step process:

  1. Pre-Division at Vertices: Before rasterization, each vertex attribute \(A\) is divided by its clip-space homogeneous coordinate \(w\), producing \(\frac{A}{w}\). The reciprocal \(\frac{1}{w}\) is also computed for each vertex.
  2. Linear Screen-Space Interpolation: The rasterizer linearly interpolates both \(\frac{A}{w}\) and \(\frac{1}{w}\) across the 2D surface of the primitive using standard barycentric coordinates.
  3. Reconstruction per Fragment: At each fragment, the interpolated attribute is divided by the interpolated reciprocal of \(w\):

\[A_{\text{correct}} = \frac{\left(\frac{A}{w}\right)_{\text{interpolated}}}{\left(\frac{1}{w}\right)_{\text{interpolated}}}\]

This per-fragment division cancels out the non-linear effects of the perspective transformation, mapping the interpolated values precisely to their correct geometric positions in 3D space. Without this step, graphics hardware cannot accurately represent depth foreshortening on planar surfaces.
