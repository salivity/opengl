Low-Precision Lighting Issues in OpenGL ES

Computing lighting equations in OpenGL ES requires balancing rendering performance with visual fidelity, particularly when targeting mobile GPUs with limited resources. Utilizing low-precision data types—such as lowp or mediump—can significantly boost frame rates and reduce bandwidth, but it often introduces severe numerical artifacts into illumination models. This article outlines the primary precision issues that arise when calculating lighting equations with reduced-precision variables, including color banding, normalization errors, specular collapse, and distance attenuation breakdown.

Color Banding and Quantization

The most visible consequence of low precision is color banding, or posterization. In OpenGL ES, the lowp qualifier often maps to an 8-bit fixed-point representation or a 10-bit float, providing a very narrow dynamic range. When computing smooth color transitions—such as standard Lambertian diffuse gradients (\(N \cdot L\))—the limited bit depth cannot represent the subtle, continuous fractional steps between neighboring pixels. Instead of a smooth gradation from light to shadow, the output snaps into noticeable, stepped bands of solid color.

Normalization and Dot Product Drift

Accurate lighting calculations depend on unit-length vectors for surface normals (\(N\)), light directions (\(L\)), and view vectors (\(V\)). In low precision:

  • Vector Length Distortions: Calculating a vector's length requires squaring components, summing them, and computing the square root. In lowp (and occasionally mediump for large vectors), the intermediate squared terms can suffer from underflow or loss of fractional precision, yielding inaccurate magnitudes.
  • Faulty Normalization: When normal vectors deviate from unit length due to coarse arithmetic, the subsequent dot products (\(N \cdot L\) or \(N \cdot H\)) produce values outside the intended \([0.0, 1.0]\) range. This leads to artificial brightening, blackened patches on curved surfaces, or sudden terminator lines where light abruptly cuts off.

Specular Highlight Collapse

Specular components, such as those in the Phong or Blinn-Phong reflection models, rely on raising a dot product to an exponent: \((N \cdot H)^\alpha\).

Low-precision variables struggle significantly with high exponents:

  1. Underflow: If the base value is slightly less than \(1.0\), raising it to a power like \(32\) or \(64\) results in extremely small fractions that easily underflow to zero in lowp or low-end mediump representations. This causes specular highlights to shrink drastically or vanish entirely.
  2. Aliasing and Shimmering: Small changes in vertex positions or interpolated fragment values cause large jumps in the exponentiated result. When combined with low precision, specular spots flicker, exhibit jagged pixelated edges, or pop abruptly across frames as the camera moves.

Attenuation and Distance Overflow

Point and spot lights rely on attenuation factors calculated using the distance (\(d\)) between the surface and the light source, typically using quadratic formulas such as:

\[\text{Attenuation} = \frac{1.0}{k_c + k_l d + k_q d^2}\]

In systems using half-precision (mediump), the maximum representable value is typically \(65,504\). If world-space coordinates are large, the squared distance \(d^2\) rapidly exceeds this limit, resulting in arithmetic overflow. An overflow results in an infinity or NaN (Not a Number), producing visual artifacts such as black holes, harsh circular boundaries, or entirely unlit geometry. In lowp, where the maximum range can be as low as \(2.0\), computing world-space distances is practically impossible.

Mitigation Strategies

To resolve these issues without sacrificing mobile performance:

  • Reserve highp for world-space positions, depth values, and distance calculations to prevent attenuation overflow.
  • Use mediump for normalized vectors (normals, tangents, directions) and dot products to preserve smooth gradients.
  • Restrict lowp strictly to final, clamped color outputs or simple, pre-baked tint operations where dynamic range constraints do not cause visible artifacts.
