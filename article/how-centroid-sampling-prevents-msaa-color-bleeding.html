How Centroid Sampling Prevents MSAA Color Bleeding

Multi-Sample Anti-Aliasing (MSAA) improves edge rendering quality by testing coverage at multiple sub-pixel locations while typically executing the fragment shader only once per pixel. However, standard MSAA evaluates fragment attributes at the pixel center, which can lead to color bleeding and texture-wrapping artifacts if that center lies outside the primitive. Centroid sampling resolves this issue in OpenGL by forcing the GPU to evaluate varying attributes at a location guaranteed to be covered by the primitive, eliminating the out-of-bounds extrapolation that causes visual artifacts.

The Cause of MSAA Color Bleeding

In standard MSAA, rasterization decouples geometric coverage from fragment shader execution. A pixel can contain multiple sample points (such as 2x, 4x, or 8x). If at least one of these sample points is covered by a triangle, the fragment shader executes to compute a color for the pixel.

By default, OpenGL evaluates interpolated fragment inputs (such as texture coordinates, normals, and colors) at the geometric center of the pixel. When a triangle clips a pixel without covering its center, the fragment shader still runs, but it evaluates attributes at that uncovered center.

Evaluating attributes outside the boundary of the triangle requires mathematical extrapolation rather than interpolation. For texture coordinates, this extrapolation pushes \((u, v)\) coordinates beyond their intended \([0, 1]\) bounds or past the borders of a sub-texture inside an atlas. When the fragment shader samples the texture using these out-of-bounds coordinates, it fetches adjacent pixels, clamps inappropriately, or wraps unexpectedly. When the resulting color is blended back into the covered sample locations, an unintended halo, dark fringe, or bleeding edge appears along the geometry's silhouette.

How Centroid Sampling Eliminates Extrapolation

Centroid sampling modifies the interpolation point of varying inputs in the graphics pipeline. Instead of blindly evaluating inputs at the pixel center, the hardware calculates a sample point bounded by the actual coverage mask.

When centroid sampling is active:

  1. The hardware inspects the coverage mask to identify which MSAA sample points are covered by the current primitive.
  2. If all samples are covered (or if the pixel center is covered), evaluation behaves normally at the pixel center.
  3. If the pixel center is not covered, the GPU shifts the attribute evaluation point to the "centroid"—a position that falls within the convex hull of the covered sample points.

Because the centroid point is guaranteed to lie within the interior of the primitive, the interpolated attributes are derived strictly through interpolation rather than extrapolation. Texture coordinates remain valid and within their intended boundaries, preventing texture lookups from bleeding adjacent or out-of-range colors onto geometry silhouettes.

Implementation in OpenGL

In OpenGL, centroid sampling is applied on a per-attribute basis using GLSL interpolation qualifiers. Both the vertex shader output and the fragment shader input must be declared with the centroid keyword.

In the vertex shader:

centroid out vec2 vTexCoord;

In the fragment shader:

centroid in vec2 vTexCoord;

By adding the centroid qualifier to texture coordinates and other sensitive interpolators, the rasterizer avoids extrapolating attributes beyond triangle boundaries, preserving clean edges and completely suppressing color bleeding under MSAA.
