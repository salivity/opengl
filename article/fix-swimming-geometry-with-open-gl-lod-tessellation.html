Fix Swimming Geometry with OpenGL LOD Tessellation

Dynamic level of detail (LOD) tessellation in OpenGL eliminates swimming geometry artifacts by replacing abrupt discrete mesh swaps with smooth, continuous mesh density transitions during camera movement. Swimming artifacts typically arise when discrete LOD transitions or camera-relative displacement sampling cause vertices and procedural details to visibly crawl across a surface. By leveraging OpenGL’s hardware tessellation pipeline—specifically using fractional spacing in the Tessellation Evaluation Shader (TES) and continuous distance metrics in the Tessellation Control Shader (TCS)—geometry scales continuously and maintains spatial stability relative to the world, completely removing visual popping and geometric crawl.

The Mechanics of Swimming Geometry

Swimming geometry occurs when a surface's vertices appear to slide, jitter, or crawl as the camera translates or rotates. In traditional discrete LOD systems, meshes snap between fixed levels of detail, causing abrupt structural jumps. When combined with displacement mapping or procedural noise (such as terrain heightmaps), slight changes in camera position cause the underlying vertex sampling grid to shift unevenly across the height field. Because vertices are reallocated non-uniformly relative to the features they represent, the high-frequency features appear to "swim" through the mesh rather than remaining anchored in world space.

Fractional Spacing in the Tessellation Evaluation Shader

The primary solution to avoid popping between discrete states in OpenGL is fractional tessellation spacing. Standard integer tessellation forces the GPU to round tessellation levels to the nearest whole number, causing immediate structural snaps when a threshold is crossed.

OpenGL provides two continuous spacing modes declared in the Tessellation Evaluation Shader:

  • fractional_even_spacing
  • fractional_odd_spacing

By defining the primitive generation mode with fractional spacing (e.g., layout(triangles, fractional_odd_spacing, ccw) in;), the tessellation primitive generator smoothly scales new segments into existence. When the tessellation level increases, new vertex pairs fade in from an edge or point with an initial length of zero and gradually expand to full size, preventing any visible popping or sliding across camera frames.

Continuous LOD Calculation in the Tessellation Control Shader

To take advantage of fractional spacing, the Tessellation Control Shader must compute tessellation factors (gl_TessLevelOuter and gl_TessLevelInner) using continuous, floating-point functions rather than quantized distance buckets.

  1. Edge-Based Distance Evaluation: Instead of calculating a single LOD factor per patch based on the patch center, compute tessellation levels independently for each patch edge. Calculate the midpoint of each edge in world space, measure the distance from that midpoint to the camera position, and map that distance smoothly to a tessellation factor using a linear or power curve:

    \[\text{TessLevel} = \text{clamp}\left(\frac{\text{MaxDistance} - \text{Distance}}{\text{MaxDistance} - \text{MinDistance}}, 0.0, 1.0\right) \times (\text{MaxTess} - \text{MinTess}) + \text{MinTess}\]

  2. Screen-Space Target Length: Alternatively, project the world-space bounding sphere of each edge into normalized device coordinates (NDC) to determine its actual screen-space footprint. Defining tessellation based on target pixel size ensures that geometry density dynamically balances across screen transformations without shifting the underlying spatial reference points.

Ensuring Edge Stitching and Crack Prevention

Geometric swimming can also manifest as flickering seam cracks along adjacent patches. If two adjacent patches evaluate the same shared edge differently, their edge vertices will not align, creating gaps that flash during camera motion. To prevent this:

  • Calculate edge tessellation factors using exclusively the two vertices defining that shared edge.
  • Ensure the function is symmetric so that regardless of edge winding order (\(AB\) versus \(BA\)), both patches assign the exact same value to gl_TessLevelOuter.
  • Derive inner tessellation factors (gl_TessLevelInner) as an average or maximum of the outer factors to maintain structural integrity across the interior of the patch.

World-Space Anchoring in Displacement Mapping

Even with fractional tessellation, surfaces will swim if displacement data is evaluated incorrectly. In the Tessellation Evaluation Shader, compute the final vertex positions using continuous barycentric interpolation (gl_TessCoord), followed by sampling displacement maps or procedural noise functions strictly in world-space coordinates:

  • Do not sample height functions using view-space coordinates or camera-dependent projections.
  • Anchor UVs and world-space sampling points to the static topology of the patch base.
  • Apply analytical or sampled normal vectors in the Fragment Shader derived from the undisturbed world coordinates, ensuring lighting does not oscillate as the underlying tessellation factor adjusts.

Through the combination of fractional primitive spacing, continuous edge-based factor evaluation, and world-space displacement mapping, OpenGL renders geometry that dynamically adapts its resolution to camera distance while maintaining absolute spatial stability.
