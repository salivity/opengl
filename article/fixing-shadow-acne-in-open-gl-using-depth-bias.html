Fixing Shadow Acne in OpenGL Using Depth Bias

Shadow acne is a common visual artifact in OpenGL shadow mapping characterized by false, striped self-shadowing patterns across illuminated surfaces. This article explains the underlying causes of shadow acne—primarily depth buffer quantization and geometric discretization—and demonstrates how to effectively eliminate it using static, slope-scaled depth bias techniques and OpenGL's built-in polygon offset functionality.

What Causes Shadow Acne

Shadow mapping operates in two distinct passes. First, the scene is rendered from the light's perspective into an off-screen depth texture (the shadow map). Second, the scene is rendered from the camera's perspective, where each fragment's distance to the light is compared against the corresponding depth value stored in the shadow map. If the fragment is further from the light than the stored value, it is marked as in shadow.

Shadow acne occurs primarily due to the resolution limits of the shadow map:

  1. Texel Discretization: The shadow map represents continuous 3D surfaces as discrete, flat texels. A single texel covers an area on a surface where the actual depth varies continuously. When the surface is sampled, roughly half of the fragments mapped to that texel have a depth slightly greater than the stored texel depth, while the other half have a depth slightly less. The fragments with a greater depth incorrectly register as occluded by the surface itself, creating alternating bands of light and dark pixels.
  2. Surface Inclination: As the angle between the light direction and the surface normal increases (grazing angles), a single shadow map texel stretches across a significantly larger surface area. This magnifies the depth discrepancy between the flat texel and the sloped surface.
  3. Floating-Point Precision: Limited precision in the depth buffer (e.g., standard 16-bit or 24-bit depth formats) introduces rounding errors during depth evaluation, causing fragments on flat planes to randomly fall behind the recorded depth value.

Resolving Shadow Acne with Depth Bias

The standard solution to shadow acne is applying a depth bias (an artificial offset) to separate the surface from the sampled depth value during the shadow comparison.

1. Constant Depth Bias

A constant bias subtracts a fixed value from the fragment's current depth before comparing it to the depth map:

// GLSL fragment shader
float bias = 0.005;
float shadow = (currentDepth - bias) > closestDepth ? 1.0 : 0.0;

While simple, a fixed bias has limitations. If the bias is set too low, acne persists on surfaces angled away from the light. If the bias is set too high, it introduces "Peter Panning," an artifact where shadows detach from the objects casting them, making objects appear to float.

2. Slope-Scaled Depth Bias in Shaders

To balance flat surfaces and steep angles, the bias can be scaled dynamically based on the angle between the surface normal and the light direction:

// Calculate bias based on the angle between surface normal and light direction
float bias = max(0.05 * (1.0 - dot(normal, lightDir)), 0.005);
float shadow = (currentDepth - bias) > closestDepth ? 1.0 : 0.0;

When the surface is perpendicular to the light (dot(normal, lightDir) approaches 1), a small minimum bias is applied, preventing Peter Panning. When the light strikes at a grazing angle (dot approaches 0), the bias scales up to account for the larger depth variance across individual texels.

3. Hardware Depth Bias (glPolygonOffset)

OpenGL provides a hardware-accelerated method to offset depth values directly during the shadow map generation pass via glPolygonOffset. This technique shifts the depth values written to the shadow map rather than adjusting calculations in the fragment shader.

Before rendering the shadow map pass:

glEnable(GL_POLYGON_OFFSET_FILL);
glPolygonOffset(factor, units);

// Render scene from light source...

glDisable(GL_POLYGON_OFFSET_FILL);
  • factor scales the maximum depth slope of the polygon. Polygons at steeper angles to the light receive a larger offset. Typical values range between 1.0 and 4.0.
  • units scales the minimum resolvable depth difference, providing a constant offset. Typical values range between 1.0 and 4.0.

Using glPolygonOffset ensures the depth map natively contains biased values, offloading slope calculations to the GPU rasterizer and keeping the lighting shader simpler.
