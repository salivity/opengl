Realistic Lens Flare Scattering in OpenGL Shaders

This article explores the mathematical formulations required to simulate realistic optical lens flare scattering in real-time using OpenGL fragment shaders (GLSL). It covers the geometric projection of lens ghosts along the optical axis, chromatic dispersion functions, analytical aperture diffraction modeling, halo generation, and intensity attenuation equations. Implementing these algorithms allows graphics programmers to generate physically plausible multi-element lens artifacts directly on the GPU without relying on pre-baked sprite textures.

1. Optical Axis Ghost Projection

Lens flares occur when high-intensity light reflects internally between multi-lens elements. In screen space, these reflections align along an axis passing through the 2D light source coordinate and the screen center.

Given a normalized device coordinate (NDC) light position \(\vec{p}_{\text{light}} \in [-1, 1]^2\) and the screen center \(\vec{c} = (0, 0)\), the optical axis vector \(\vec{v}\) is defined as:

\[\vec{v} = \vec{c} - \vec{p}_{\text{light}} = -\vec{p}_{\text{light}}\]

Individual ghost artifacts are distributed along this vector at scalar intervals \(s_i\). The sampling coordinate for the \(i\)-th ghost is:

\[\vec{p}_i = \vec{p}_{\text{light}} + \vec{v} \cdot s_i\]

In a GLSL fragment shader, iterating over \(N\) ghost instances samples the downsampled scene brightness threshold texture:

vec2 ghostVec = (vec2(0.0) - lightPosNDC) * u_Dispersal;
for (int i = 0; i < u_GhostCount; ++i) {
    vec2 offset = fract(lightPosNDC + ghostVec * float(i));
    // Sample texture at offset
}

2. Chromatic Aberration and Spectral Dispersion

Glass components have a refractive index \(\eta(\lambda)\) that varies with the wavelength \(\lambda\) of light (Cauchy's dispersion equation):

\[\eta(\lambda) = A + \frac{B}{\lambda^2}\]

In a fragment shader, this wavelength dependency is approximated by shifting the texture sampling offset per color channel (Red, Green, Blue) along the normalized direction vector \(\hat{v} = \frac{\vec{v}}{\|\vec{v}\|}\).

For a sampling vector \(\vec{u}\), the channel offsets are computed as:

\[\vec{u}_R = \vec{u} + \hat{v} \cdot \Delta\lambda_R\] \[\vec{u}_G = \vec{u} + \hat{v} \cdot \Delta\lambda_G\] \[\vec{u}_B = \vec{u} + \hat{v} \cdot \Delta\lambda_B\]

Where \(\Delta\lambda_R > \Delta\lambda_G > \Delta\lambda_B\) represent empirical dispersion factors.

vec3 sampleChromatic(sampler2D tex, vec2 uv, vec2 direction, vec3 distortion) {
    return vec3(
        texture(tex, uv + direction * distortion.r).r,
        texture(tex, uv + direction * distortion.g).g,
        texture(tex, uv + direction * distortion.b).b
    );
}

3. Radial Halo Formation

Halos are caused by internal cylindrical reflection or total internal reflection within curved lens borders. A halo appears as a ring centered on the light source or the optical inversion center.

The distance \(d\) from the fragment UV to the inverted light position is:

\[\vec{p}_{\text{halo}} = -\vec{p}_{\text{light}}\] \[d = \|\vec{uv} - \vec{p}_{\text{halo}}\|\]

The intensity of the halo \(I_{\text{halo}}\) is formulated using a smooth distribution function centered at a predefined radius \(r\) with width \(w\):

\[I_{\text{halo}} = \exp\left(-\frac{(d - r)^2}{2w^2}\right)\]

In GLSL, this can be efficiently calculated using smoothstep:

vec2 haloVec = normalize(vec2(0.0) - lightPosNDC) * u_HaloRadius;
float haloWeight = length(vec2(0.0) - fract(uv + haloVec));
haloWeight = smoothstep(0.0, u_HaloThickness, abs(u_HaloRadius - haloWeight));

4. Fraunhofer Diffraction and Starburst Spikes

Diffraction from the camera’s polygonal aperture produces starburst spikes radiating from the core flare. According to Fraunhofer diffraction theory, the far-field intensity pattern is the Fourier transform of the aperture function.

For an \(N\)-bladed iris, this can be modeled analytically in polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\) relative to the light center:

\[r = \|\vec{uv} - \vec{p}_{\text{light}}\|, \quad \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(uv_y - p_{y}, uv_x - p_{x})\]

The starburst intensity modulation \(M(\theta)\) is governed by:

\[M(\theta) = \left| \cos\left(\frac{N}{2} \cdot \theta + \phi \right) \right|^k\]

Where:

  • \(N\) is the number of aperture blades.
  • \(\phi\) is the camera rotation angle.
  • \(k\) controls the sharpness and narrowness of the diffraction spikes.

To account for sub-surface irregularities on the glass, a pseudo-random high-frequency noise function \(W(\theta)\) is multiplied:

\[I_{\text{diffraction}}(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{r^2 + \epsilon} \cdot M(\theta) \cdot W(\theta)\]

5. Radial Attenuation and Edge Falloff

Scattering diminishes as light diverges, governed by the inverse-square law, while physical lens hoods and barrels occlude peripheral light (vignetting).

To prevent visual pops when light sources move off-screen, a boundary attenuation factor \(A(\vec{p})\) suppresses flare intensity based on the distance of \(\vec{p}_{\text{light}}\) from the center:

\[A(\vec{p}_{\text{light}}) = 1.0 - \left(\frac{\|\vec{p}_{\text{light}}\|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^m\]

Where \(m\) defines the steepness of the falloff towards the corner of the NDC unit square.

The radial falloff of any given flare element relative to its center is calculated as:

\[F(d) = \max\left(0.0, 1.0 - \left(\frac{d}{R_{\max}}\right)^n\right)\]

Combining these formulations produces a multi-spectral, geometrically accurate scattering profile evaluated entirely within a single post-processing fragment pass.
