Smith Microfacet Shadowing in OpenGL Explained

This article explores how the Smith geometric attenuation function models microfacet self-shadowing and masking within physically based rendering (PBR) pipelines in OpenGL. Microfacet theory assumes rough surfaces consist of microscopic specular facets, many of which occlude one another depending on surface roughness and view angles. By decoupling or correlating the occlusion of incoming light rays and outgoing viewing rays, the Smith formulation provides an energy-conserving statistical approximation of surface self-occlusion that can be evaluated efficiently within standard GLSL fragment shaders.

The Physics of Masking and Shadowing

In microfacet theory (such as the Cook-Torrance specular BRDF), light reflection is governed by three primary factors: normal distribution (\(D\)), Fresnel reflectance (\(F\)), and geometric attenuation (\(G\)). As light interacts with a rough macro-surface, microscopic peaks and valleys cause two distinct forms of geometric occlusion:

  1. Shadowing: Incoming light rays from the light direction (\(L\)) are intercepted by neighboring microfacets before they can strike a given microfacet oriented toward the half-vector (\(H\)).
  2. Masking: Light that successfully reflects off an oriented microfacet toward the observer (\(V\)) is blocked by adjacent microfacets before it can leave the surface.

Without accounting for these effects, microfacet distribution functions overestimate reflected radiance at grazing angles, violating the conservation of energy and creating unnatural, overly bright silhouettes.

The Smith Formulation

The Smith formulation solves this by statistically estimating the fraction of microfacets that are visible from a given direction. Rather than evaluating complex individual micro-geometries, it derives visibility based on the roughness parameter (\(\alpha\)) and the macro-surface normal (\(N\)).

Separable Smith Model

Originally, the Smith approach assumed that shadowing and masking are independent events. The joint geometric attenuation factor \(G(L, V)\) is expressed as the product of two separate monodirectional shadowing functions, \(G_1\):

\[G(L, V) = G_1(L) \cdot G_1(V)\]

Where:

  • \(G_1(L)\) is the probability that a microfacet is illuminated (shadowing).
  • \(G_1(V)\) is the probability that a microfacet is visible to the camera (masking).

Height-Correlated Smith Model

In reality, microfacets positioned higher on the surface are simultaneously less likely to be shadowed and less likely to be masked. Modern real-time engines using OpenGL prefer the height-correlated Smith model derived by Eric Heitz, which accounts for the statistical correlation between masking and shadowing:

\[G(L, V) = \frac{2(N \cdot L)(N \cdot V)}{(N \cdot V)\sqrt{\alpha^2 + (1 - \alpha^2)(N \cdot L)^2} + (N \cdot L)\sqrt{\alpha^2 + (1 - \alpha^2)(N \cdot V)^2}}\]

This prevents the separable formulation from over-darkening rough surfaces at grazing angles.

Integration in OpenGL Shaders

In an OpenGL fragment shader implementing Cook-Torrance specular BRDF, the specular term is typically defined as:

\[f_{spec} = \frac{D(H) \cdot F(V, H) \cdot G(L, V)}{4(N \cdot L)(N \cdot V)}\]

Evaluating \(G(L, V)\) independently often involves redundant computations with the denominator \(4(N \cdot L)(N \cdot V)\). Shading languages typically combine geometric attenuation with the Cook-Torrance denominator into a single visibility term, \(V(L, V)\):

\[V(L, V) = \frac{G(L, V)}{4(N \cdot L)(N \cdot V)}\]

Using the height-correlated GGX formulation, this reduces in GLSL to:

float geometrySmithCorrelated(float NdotV, float NdotL, float roughness) {
    float a2 = roughness * roughness;
    float lambdaV = NdotL * sqrt(a2 + (1.0 - a2) * (NdotV * NdotV));
    float lambdaL = NdotV * sqrt(a2 + (1.0 - a2) * (NdotL * NdotL));
    return 0.5 / (lambdaV + lambdaL);
}

Alternatively, many real-time implementations use the Schlick approximation of the Smith model, known as Smith-Schlick:

float geometrySchlickGGX(float NdotV, float roughness) {
    float r = roughness + 1.0;
    float k = (r * r) / 8.0; // Optimized for direct lighting

    float denom = NdotV * (1.0 - k) + k;
    return NdotV / denom;
}

float geometrySmith(float NdotV, float NdotL, float roughness) {
    float ggx1 = geometrySchlickGGX(NdotV, roughness);
    float ggx2 = geometrySchlickGGX(NdotL, roughness);
    return ggx1 * ggx2;
}

Behavioral Characteristics

  • Grazing Angles: As the angle between the surface normal and the view vector (\(N \cdot V\)) approaches zero, \(G_1(V)\) drops toward zero, aggressively attenuating the reflection. This directly balances the Fresnel factor (\(F\)), which surges toward 1.0 at grazing angles, avoiding unrealistic glare.
  • Roughness Scaling: On smoother surfaces (\(\alpha \approx 0\)), self-occlusion is negligible except at extreme edges. On rougher surfaces (\(\alpha \approx 1\)), micro-level peaks and valleys cause substantial light scattering, resulting in lower overall specular intensity and a broader, softer highlight spread across the surface.
