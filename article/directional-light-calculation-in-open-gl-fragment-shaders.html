Directional Light Calculation in OpenGL Fragment Shaders

Directional light calculation simulates illumination from a light source located infinitely far away, producing parallel light rays across an entire 3D scene. This article explains the core principles of directional lighting and details how its constant direction vector is managed, transformed, and processed within an OpenGL fragment shader using standard reflection models.

Understanding Directional Light

In computer graphics, a directional light models sources like the sun, where the distance to the scene is so vast that the light rays are effectively parallel to each other. Unlike point lights or spotlights, a directional light has no specific position in the world; it is defined entirely by its direction, color, and intensity. Because the light rays are parallel, the angle of incoming light is identical for every surface in the scene, and there is typically no distance-based attenuation.

The Constant Direction Vector

The orientation of a directional light is represented by a 3D vector. In lighting calculations, this vector represents the direction towards the light source from the surface, often referred to as \(\vec{L}\).

If the light source is defined by the direction in which its rays travel (for example, pointing down and forward: vec3(0.0, -1.0, -1.0)), the vector must be inverted so it points from the fragment toward the source:

\[\vec{L} = -\text{lightDirection}\]

Because this direction does not change based on fragment position, it is passed into the OpenGL pipeline as a uniform variable (uniform vec3), maintaining a constant value across every fragment drawn during a draw call.

Coordinate Space Considerations

For accurate calculations, the light direction vector and the surface normal must exist within the same coordinate space—typically either World Space or View (Eye) Space.

  • World Space: The surface normal is transformed using the model matrix (or normal matrix), and the light direction remains as defined globally.
  • View Space: The light direction vector is multiplied by the view matrix (ignoring translation) so that the camera sits at the origin, simplifying specular calculations.

Processing in the OpenGL Fragment Stage

Inside the fragment shader, the directional light vector interacts with the surface normal to determine the final fragment color, typically via the Blinn-Phong reflection model consisting of ambient, diffuse, and specular components.

1. Vector Normalization

Interpolation across rasterized triangles can alter vector lengths. Therefore, both the surface normal and the light vector must be normalized to unit length:

vec3 norm = normalize(fs_in.Normal);
vec3 lightDir = normalize(-directionalLight.direction);

2. Diffuse Component

Diffuse shading utilizes Lambert's Cosine Law, which scales brightness based on the angle between the surface normal (\(\vec{N}\)) and the incoming light direction (\(\vec{L}\)). This is computed using the dot product:

float diff = max(dot(norm, lightDir), 0.0);
vec3 diffuse = diff * directionalLight.color * texture(material.diffuse, fs_in.TexCoords).rgb;

If the light hits the surface head-on (\(\vec{N} \cdot \vec{L} = 1.0\)), the diffuse intensity is maximal. If the surface faces away from the light, the dot product becomes negative, which is clamped to 0.0 by the max function to prevent reverse illumination.

3. Specular Component (Blinn-Phong)

The specular highlight calculates the reflection seen by the camera. The Blinn-Phong model computes a halfway vector (\(\vec{H}\)) between the light direction (\(\vec{L}\)) and the view direction (\(\vec{V}\)):

vec3 viewDir = normalize(viewPos - fs_in.FragPos);
vec3 halfwayDir = normalize(lightDir + viewDir);
float spec = pow(max(dot(norm, halfwayDir), 0.0), material.shininess);
vec3 specular = spec * directionalLight.color * texture(material.specular, fs_in.TexCoords).rgb;

4. Final Color Combination

The ambient, diffuse, and specular results are summed to generate the final fragment output:

vec3 ambient = directionalLight.ambient * texture(material.diffuse, fs_in.TexCoords).rgb;
FragColor = vec4(ambient + diffuse + specular, 1.0);

Because the direction vector remains constant across all fragments, directional light calculations in OpenGL are computationally lightweight compared to localized lights, requiring no attenuation curves or per-fragment distance math.
