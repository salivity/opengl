Derivative Continuity and Branching in OpenGL

In modern GPU pipelines, partial derivative calculation continuity refers to the spatial consistency required across neighboring fragments to compute rate-of-change functions like dFdx and dFdy. Modern GPUs calculate these derivatives by executing fragment shaders in 2x2 pixel groups (quads) and differencing values between adjacent pixels. When control flow branches diverge across a quad—meaning some pixels take a branch while their neighbors do not—this continuity is broken. This divergence leads to undefined derivative calculations, frequently causing visual artifacts such as severe texture shimmering or incorrect level-of-detail (LOD) selection.

The Mechanism of Derivative Calculations in GLSL

OpenGL Shading Language (GLSL) provides built-in functions such as dFdx, dFdy, and fwidth to compute the rate of change of a variable with respect to window coordinates. These derivatives are foundational not only for custom procedural effects and screen-space normal computations, but also for implicit texture operations like mipmap level selection during a standard texture() call.

Hardware GPUs do not compute analytical derivatives. Instead, fragment shading executes in lockstep over SIMD/SIMT groups arranged in 2x2 pixel quads. The GPU evaluates screen-space partial derivatives via finite differences:

  • dFdx(val) is computed by subtracting the value of val in the left pixel from the value of val in the right pixel.
  • dFdy(val) is computed by subtracting the value of val in the bottom pixel from the value of val in the top pixel.

Derivative calculation continuity requires that every fragment inside the 2x2 quad executes the exact same expression in lockstep, ensuring all four sample points are valid and active.

How Control Flow Branching Breaks Continuity

Control flow branching breaks derivative continuity when the conditional statement is non-uniform—meaning it evaluates differently for fragments within the same 2x2 quad.

When execution diverges:

  1. Inactive Fragments: The GPU masks out the fragments that evaluated the condition to false while executing the true block, and vice versa.
  2. Missing Operands: If an explicit derivative (dFdx) or an implicit derivative (like a mipmapped texture() sample) occurs inside a divergent branch, the operation requires values from adjacent pixels in the quad. However, the adjacent pixels may not have executed that branch and thus do not possess a valid, updated value for the variable being differentiated.
  3. Undefined Behavior: Because the neighbor's state inside the branch is uninitialized, stale, or discarded, the finite difference calculation fails. The GLSL specification defines calculating derivatives inside non-uniform control flow as undefined behavior.

Consequences and Artifacts

The primary manifestation of broken derivative continuity occurs in texture mapping. Standard sampling functions (texture()) rely on derivatives to calculate the footprint of a pixel in texture space to pick the correct mipmap level.

If derivatives become undefined or spike due to differencing against uninitialized neighbor data:

  • The GPU may calculate an artificially massive derivative, forcing the texture sampler to immediately drop to the lowest-resolution mipmap level (causing localized blurriness).
  • Conversely, it may calculate zero or negative derivatives, causing the GPU to sample the highest-resolution mipmap at a distance, resulting in severe aliasing, moiré patterns, and shimmering.

Solutions and Best Practices

To preserve derivative continuity and avoid rendering defects, developers use specific patterns:

  • Hoisting Derivative Computations: Calculate the derivative or sample the texture prior to the divergent branch, storing the result in a variable that can be consumed conditionally later.
  • Explicit LOD and Gradients: When texture sampling inside divergent branches is unavoidable, replace standard sampling with functions that bypass implicit quad differencing:
    • textureLod(): Manually specifies the exact mipmap level to sample, eliminating the need for screen-space derivatives.
    • textureGrad(): Allows explicit passing of precomputed gradient vectors (dPdx and dPdy) derived outside the divergent block.
