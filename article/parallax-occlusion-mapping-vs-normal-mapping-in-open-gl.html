Parallax Occlusion Mapping vs Normal Mapping in OpenGL

Parallax occlusion mapping is an advanced shading technique that simulates complex geometric displacement and depth on flat surfaces without adding extra polygons. While standard normal mapping alters surface lighting to mimic minor details, parallax occlusion mapping calculates visual depth, self-occlusion, and motion parallax through an internal ray-marching algorithm implemented directly within fragment shaders. This article explores how parallax occlusion mapping operates and how it significantly expands upon standard normal mapping in an OpenGL rendering pipeline.

Standard Normal Mapping and Its Limitations

Normal mapping is a standard technique in modern real-time rendering. In OpenGL, an artist bakes fine geometric details from a high-poly mesh into a 2D RGB texture known as a normal map. Inside the fragment shader, these RGB values are decoded into normal vectors (typically mapped from the \([0, 1]\) range to \([-1, 1]\) in tangent space). The fragment shader then uses these per-pixel normals instead of the geometric surface normal to compute lighting models such as Blinn-Phong or physically based rendering (PBR).

While computationally cheap and effective for subtle surface bumps, normal mapping has distinct limitations:

  • No Geometric Parallax: As the camera moves across the surface, details do not shift relative to one another.
  • Flat Grazing Angles: When viewed at sharp angles, the illusion breaks completely because the silhouette remains perfectly flat.
  • No Self-Occlusion: High points on the texture cannot obscure low points behind them.

What is Parallax Occlusion Mapping?

Parallax Occlusion Mapping (POM) resolves these limitations by incorporating a height map (often packed into the alpha channel of a normal map or bound as a separate texture) alongside the surface normals. Instead of simply reading the texture coordinate supplied by the vertex stage, POM performs an iterative ray-marching routine in the fragment shader to determine which point of the height field the camera is actually looking at.

When a fragment is processed, a ray is projected from the camera's eye position into the height field in tangent space. The shader steps along this view ray, comparing the ray's depth with the height sampled from the height map. Once the ray intersects the height field, the shader interpolates between the step before and after the intersection to find a precise texture coordinate offset.

How POM Expands Upon Normal Mapping

Parallax occlusion mapping does not replace normal mapping; rather, it augments it by dynamically calculating correct UV offsets before normal sampling and lighting occur.

1. Motion Parallax

Because texture coordinates are displaced based on the angle between the surface normal and the view direction, features appear to have genuine depth. Features closer to the viewer shift faster than recessed features, creating real motion parallax as the camera moves.

2. True Self-Occlusion

In standard normal mapping, a deep crevice receives the same UV coordinate regardless of angle. In POM, if a raised edge blocks the line of sight into a crevice, the ray-march terminates on the raised edge. This causes raised features to realistically occlude lower details at shallow viewing angles.

3. Self-Shadowing

POM shaders can perform a secondary ray-march from the calculated surface intersection point toward the light source. If any height field geometry intersects this light ray, the fragment is determined to be in shadow. This generates dynamic self-shadows cast by micro-geometry onto itself, something impossible with standard normal mapping.

Implementing POM in the OpenGL Pipeline

Integrating POM into an OpenGL shader requires moving lighting and view computations into tangent space to align with the height map data:

  1. Vertex Shader Preparation: The vertex shader computes the Tangent, Bitangent, and Normal (TBN) matrix and uses it to transform the view vector (camera position minus vertex position) from world space into tangent space. Passing the tangent-space view vector to the fragment shader ensures the ray-marching algorithm operates in texture coordinate space.
  2. Ray Marching in the Fragment Shader: The fragment shader divides the height map into a set number of layers (often scaling dynamically based on the view angle to optimize performance). It advances along the tangent-space view ray until the ray depth exceeds the sampled height value.
  3. Occlusion Interpolation: The shader linearly interpolates between the height values of the last two steps to produce an accurate texture coordinate without requiring extremely high sample counts.
  4. Sampling and Lighting: The modified texture coordinate is then used to sample the normal map, albedo map, and roughness maps. The rest of the lighting calculations proceed as normal, resulting in a three-dimensional appearance on an otherwise flat polygon.
