Simulating Rayleigh and Mie Scattering in OpenGL Shaders

This article provides an overview of atmospheric rendering through the simulation of Rayleigh and Mie scattering models within an OpenGL pipeline. It details the physical principles behind how light interacts with atmospheric particles, breaks down the mathematical equations governing phase and attenuation, and demonstrates how to structure an efficient ray-marching algorithm inside a GLSL fragment shader to generate realistic dynamic skies.

Fundamentals of Atmospheric Scattering

Atmospheric scattering occurs when photons emitted by a light source (primarily the Sun) collide with atmospheric particles and are redirected toward an observer. To recreate this in computer graphics, two distinct physical models are combined:

  • Rayleigh Scattering: Governs interactions with particles much smaller than the wavelength of visible light, such as nitrogen and oxygen molecules. Scattering intensity is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength (\(\lambda^{-4}\)). Shorter wavelengths (blue light) scatter significantly more than longer wavelengths (red light), which produces the characteristic blue daytime sky and the warm amber hues of sunrise and sunset when light travels through a thicker atmospheric path.
  • Mie Scattering: Governs interactions with particles roughly equal to or larger than the wavelength of light, such as aerosols, dust, pollen, and water droplets. Mie scattering is mostly wavelength-independent, scattering all colors relatively equally to create a whitish haze. It exhibits a strong forward-scattering bias, producing a bright glow directly around the sun disc.

Mathematical Formulation

The simulation solves the radiative transfer equation along a view ray using three primary components: density distribution, optical depth, and phase functions.

1. Atmospheric Density

Atmospheric density decreases exponentially with altitude. Given a point at altitude \(h\) above the planet's surface:

\[\rho_R(h) = \exp\left(-\frac{h}{H_R}\right)\] \[\rho_M(h) = \exp\left(-\frac{h}{H_M}\right)\]

where \(H_R\) (~8 km) and \(H_M\) (~1.2 km) represent the scale heights for Rayleigh and Mie particles, defining the altitude at which density drops by a factor of \(e\).

2. Transmittance and Optical Depth

The attenuation of light traveling between two points \(A\) and \(B\) follows the Beer-Lambert law:

\[T(A, B) = \exp\left( - \sum_{i \in \{R, M\}} \beta_i \int_A^B \rho_i(h(s)) \, ds \right)\]

where \(\beta_R\) is the Rayleigh scattering coefficient vector (RGB) and \(\beta_M\) is the Mie scattering/extinction coefficient.

3. Phase Functions

Phase functions describe the angular distribution of scattered light relative to the incident light direction \(\theta\):

  • Rayleigh Phase Function: \[P_R(\theta) = \frac{3}{16\pi} (1 + \cos^2\theta)\]
  • Mie Phase Function (Henyey-Greenstein approximation): \[P_M(\theta, g) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \frac{1 - g^2}{(1 + g^2 - 2g\cos\theta)^{3/2}}\] The asymmetry factor \(g \in [0.76, 0.99]\) controls the intensity of forward scattering.

Framing the Simulation in an OpenGL Shader

In an OpenGL pipeline, atmospheric scattering is commonly evaluated in a screen-space post-processing pass or rendered across a full-screen quad mapped to the camera's far plane. The fragment shader implements a numerical integration technique known as ray marching.

Step 1: Ray-Sphere Intersection

The shader reconstructs the normalized world-space view ray \(\vec{d}\) from the camera position \(\vec{c}\). It then computes the intersections with two concentric spheres representing the planet surface (radius \(R_p\)) and the outer atmospheric boundary (radius \(R_{atmo}\)).

  • If the ray points away from the atmosphere, the background color is returned.
  • If the ray intersects the atmosphere, the integration range \([t_{start}, t_{end}]\) is determined along \(\vec{d}\).

Step 2: Primary Ray Marching (View Ray)

The segment between \(t_{start}\) and \(t_{end}\) is divided into \(N\) discrete samples (typically 16 to 64 steps for real-time performance). At each primary sample point \(P_i\):

  1. Compute the local altitude \(h = \|P_i\| - R_p\) to calculate local densities \(\rho_R(h)\) and \(\rho_M(h)\).
  2. Accumulate the optical depth from the camera to \(P_i\).

Step 3: Secondary Ray Marching (Light Ray)

From sample point \(P_i\), a secondary ray is cast toward the directional light source (Sun) to calculate how much sunlight reaches \(P_i\) without being scattered away:

  1. Determine the intersection of the light ray with the top of the atmosphere.
  2. March along this path with \(M\) secondary samples (typically 4 to 8 steps).
  3. Integrate the optical depths to obtain the total transmittance \(T(P_i, \text{Sun})\).

Step 4: In-Scattering Accumulation

The total light reaching the camera is integrated by multiplying the attenuated sunlight by the local particle densities and the step length \(ds\):

\[I_{step} = T(\text{Camera}, P_i) \cdot T(P_i, \text{Sun}) \cdot (\rho_R(h)\beta_R + \rho_M(h)\beta_M) \, ds\]

Step 5: Applying Phase Functions and Sun Intensity

Outside the loop, the phase functions are evaluated once using the angle between the view ray and the sun direction (\(\cos\theta = \vec{d} \cdot \vec{L}\)). The accumulated Rayleigh and Mie sums are weighted by their respective phase values, multiplied by the incoming solar irradiance \(I_{sun}\), and combined into the final fragment output:

\[\text{Final Color} = I_{sun} \cdot \left[ P_R(\theta)\beta_R \sum I_R + P_M(\theta, g)\beta_M \sum I_M \right]\]

Performance Optimization Strategies

Direct nested ray marching requires \(N \times M\) evaluations per fragment, which can be computationally expensive at high display resolutions. Real-time implementations in OpenGL typically employ optimizations:

  • Precomputed Look-Up Tables (LUTs): Systems like the Bruneton model precompute multi-scattering and optical depth integrals into 2D or 3D textures, reducing per-frame fragment shader workloads to a few texture lookups.
  • Half-Resolution Rendering: Running the atmospheric pass at half or quarter resolution and upsampling to full resolution using a bilateral depth-aware filter significantly minimizes fill-rate overhead.
