Screen-Space Planar Reflections in OpenGL

Screen-space planar reflection mapping for flat surfaces, such as water planes, is a hybrid rendering technique in OpenGL that generates dynamic reflections by repurposing data already rendered to the screen buffer. By constraining the reflection calculations to a single mathematical plane, the technique evaluates screen-space color and depth buffers to determine what reflected geometry is visible from the camera's perspective. This article explains the geometric theory, the screen-space raymarching and reprojection mechanics, and the implementation pipeline used to render realistic water surfaces efficiently in OpenGL.

The Mathematics of the Planar Surface

A flat surface like a water plane is defined in world space by an infinite plane equation:

\[Ax + By + Cz + D = 0\]

where \(\mathbf{n} = [A, B, C]^T\) represents the normalized surface normal vector, and \(D\) represents the distance from the world origin to the plane.

When light hits this plane, the view vector from the camera reflects across the surface normal according to the law of reflection:

\[\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{V} - 2(\mathbf{V} \cdot \mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}\]

Because the plane is uniform and two-dimensional, reflections can be simplified compared to arbitrary, curved geometry. The reflected world-space ray originating from the water surface is mapped directly into normalized device coordinates (NDC) to search for intersections within the existing scene color and depth buffers.

The Screen-Space Reflection Mechanism

Unlike traditional planar reflections that require a secondary rendering pass with an inverted virtual camera, screen-space planar reflections (SSPR) extract color data directly from the primary camera's color buffer. The algorithm proceeds through the following steps:

  1. Scene Pre-pass: The opaque scene geometry (terrain, buildings, skybox) is rendered into a standard Framebuffer Object (FBO) containing a color texture and a depth texture.
  2. Water Surface Rasterization: The water plane is drawn using a custom fragment shader.
  3. Ray Generation: For each fragment on the water surface, the shader reconstructs the world-space position and constructs a reflected view ray pointing into the scene.
  4. Screen-Space Raymarching: The shader steps along the reflected ray in screen space, comparing the ray's depth against the depth value stored in the depth buffer:
    • At each step, the 3D ray position is projected into NDC space (using the projection matrix) and converted to UV texture coordinates (\([0, 1]\) range).
    • The depth texture is sampled at these UV coordinates.
    • If the ray's current depth is greater than the sampled depth, an intersection (hit) is registered.
  5. Color Retrieval: Once a hit is detected, the color buffer is sampled at the hit coordinates to fetch the reflected color.

Direct Planar Reprojection (SSPR)

For strictly flat planes, an alternative to iterative raymarching is direct screen-space reprojection. Because every point reflected across a plane mirrors to an exact conjugate point:

  1. Visible scene pixels are reflected across the water plane geometry mathematically.
  2. The reflected points are reprojected back into screen space to determine which pixel on the water plane receives that reflected color.
  3. A compute shader or a scatter pass writes these colors into a planar reflection texture.
  4. The water shader reads this texture using standard UV coordinates during the forward pass.

This eliminates raymarching artifacts, avoids precision misses on thin geometry, and operates in constant time.

Handling Water Surface Perturbations

Calm water acts as a mirror, but real water surfaces feature waves and ripples. In OpenGL, this is handled through normal mapping:

  • A normal map (or combination of animated flow maps) perturbs the flat plane's geometric normal vector per fragment.
  • The perturbed normal alters the reflection vector before screen-space sampling occurs.
  • In practice, this introduces an offset to the sampled screen-space UV coordinates:
vec2 distortedUV = hitUV + (normal.xz * distortionFactor);
vec4 reflectionColor = texture(screenColorTexture, distortedUV);

Limitations and Fallback Strategies

Screen-space planar reflection relies entirely on visible pixels within the viewport. This introduces specific graphical limitations:

  • Off-Screen Missing Information: If the geometry being reflected is occluded or outside the camera's field of view, the depth search fails.
  • Edge Fading: To prevent harsh cutoffs where reflected rays leave the screen boundary, a screen-edge attenuation factor is applied, fading the reflection out near the borders of the viewport.
  • Fallbacks: When a ray fails to find a valid intersection in the depth buffer, the shader falls back to an environment cubemap or skybox color.

Final Shading Integration

The final fragment color on the water plane is synthesized by combining the screen-space reflection with refraction and surface properties using the Fresnel effect:

\[F = F_0 + (1 - F_0)(1 - \mathbf{V} \cdot \mathbf{n})^5\]

The Fresnel factor determines the ratio between the refracted scene color (sampled from below the water plane) and the screen-space reflected color, producing physically grounded reflections that grow more intense at grazing viewing angles.
