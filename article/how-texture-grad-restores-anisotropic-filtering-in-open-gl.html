How textureGrad Restores Anisotropic Filtering in OpenGL

Gradient texture sampling using the textureGrad function in OpenGL enables developers to manually supply directional derivatives for texture lookups instead of relying on automatic hardware evaluations. In standard rendering pipelines, non-uniform coordinate operations—such as texture atlasing, cubic mapping, and wrapped procedural coordinates—disrupt the GPU's automatic screen-space derivative calculations, resulting in mipmapping artifacts or the complete loss of anisotropic filtering. By explicitly defining the rate of change of texture coordinates along the screen’s horizontal and vertical axes, textureGrad eliminates derivative discontinuities and restores hardware-accelerated anisotropic filtering to its full fidelity.

The Mechanics of Automatic Texture Sampling

Under standard conditions, calling texture(sampler, uv) delegates mipmap selection and anisotropic footprint calculation entirely to the GPU hardware. Graphics hardware evaluates fragments in \(2 \times 2\) pixel blocks (quads). To determine the appropriate Level of Detail (LOD) and sampling shape, the GPU automatically calculates implicit screen-space partial derivatives:

  • dFdx(uv): Rate of change of texture coordinates across adjacent horizontal pixels.
  • dFdy(uv): Rate of change of texture coordinates across adjacent vertical pixels.

These derivative vectors define a parallelogram in texture space representing the projection of a single screen pixel. When the projection is elongated, anisotropic filtering samples multiple points along the major axis of this footprint to preserve high-frequency details at oblique viewing angles.

The Problem: Derivative Discontinuities

Automatic derivative calculation fails when texture coordinates change discontinuously across adjacent pixels in a quad. Common scenarios include:

  1. Coordinate Wrapping: When UV coordinates wrap from 1.0 back to 0.0, the difference across two adjacent pixels in a quad is near 1.0 rather than a minute fraction.
  2. Texture Atlases: Clamping or repeating sub-textures within an atlas creates sharp coordinate borders.
  3. Non-Uniform Control Flow: Sampling inside dynamic loops or divergent conditional branches makes cross-quad finite differences undefined or illegal.

When a derivative spike occurs, the GPU interprets the large delta as an indicator that the surface is extremely far away. Consequently, the hardware selects the lowest-resolution mipmap, creating a prominent blurry line or seam along the boundary.

The Limitation of textureLod

A standard workaround for seam artifacts is textureLod(sampler, uv, lod). While textureLod avoids derivative evaluation by forcing an explicit scalar mipmap level, it discards directional footprint information. Without independent horizontal and vertical derivative vectors, the GPU cannot determine the degree of surface slant. Consequently, textureLod completely disables anisotropic filtering, leaving slanted surfaces blurry even if the seam artifact is removed.

How textureGrad Restores Anisotropic Filtering

textureGrad resolves both derivative spikes and anisotropic degradation by accepting explicit gradient vectors:

vec4 textureGrad(sampler2D sampler, vec2 P, vec2 dPdx, vec2 dPdy);

Instead of letting hardware compute finite differences across discontinuities, the shader explicitly provides:

  • dPdx: The rate of change of the coordinates in the screen-space X direction.
  • dPdy: The rate of change of the coordinates in the screen-space Y direction.

Because these gradients are directional vectors rather than a single scalar LOD, they fully describe the shape, size, and orientation of the pixel's projection on the texture.

To restore anisotropic filtering without seam artifacts:

  1. Calculate Continuous Derivatives: Compute derivatives on uninterrupted coordinates prior to wrapping or projection modifications (for example, using dFdx(raw_uv) and dFdy(raw_uv) before applying a fract() operation).
  2. Pass Filtered Footprints: Supply the continuous gradient vectors directly to textureGrad.

By receiving valid, continuous vectors, the texture unit correctly constructs the anisotropic sampling ellipse. The hardware samples the appropriate number of texels along the anisotropic line of projection, maintaining sharp, perspective-correct textures across seams and high-angle surfaces.
