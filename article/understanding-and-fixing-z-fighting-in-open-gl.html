Understanding and Fixing Z-Fighting in OpenGL

Z-fighting is a visual artifact that occurs when two or more surfaces share nearly identical depth values, causing them to flicker as the graphics hardware struggles to determine which surface is in front. This article explains the underlying mathematical and hardware causes of z-fighting in OpenGL, details the role of non-linear depth precision in standard perspective projections, and demonstrates how properly configuring the camera frustum's near clipping plane effectively mitigates the issue.

What Causes Z-Fighting?

Z-fighting stems from the finite precision of the depth buffer (Z-buffer), typically 16-bit, 24-bit, or 32-bit. When the GPU rasterizes primitives, it computes an interpolated depth value for each fragment and compares it against the value stored in the depth buffer to resolve visibility.

When coplanar or nearly coplanar polygons overlap in screen space, their calculated depth values can be practically identical. Due to floating-point rounding errors and interpolation inaccuracies across pixels, some fragments of Polygon A are evaluated as closer than Polygon B, while adjacent fragments evaluate the opposite way. As the camera moves or objects rotate, these precision variations change frame-by-frame, producing severe visual flickering.

Non-Linear Depth Distribution in OpenGL

In OpenGL, a standard perspective projection matrix maps eye-space depth values (\(z_e\)) to normalized device coordinates (\(z_{ndc}\)), which range from \(-1.0\) to \(1.0\) (or \(0.0\) to \(1.0\) in clip space). This transformation is inherently non-linear because depth is divided by the camera-space \(w\)-coordinate (which is proportional to \(-z_e\)).

The resulting depth value is proportional to \(\frac{1}{z_e}\). Because of this reciprocal relationship:

  • A massive portion of available depth buffer precision is allocated to regions immediately adjacent to the near clipping plane (\(n\)).
  • Very little precision remains for the vast majority of the viewable scene stretching out toward the far clipping plane (\(f\)).

For example, with a near plane at \(0.01\) and a far plane at \(1000.0\), more than half of the distinct depth buffer values are consumed within the first few units of the camera, leaving almost no precision to distinguish overlapping geometry situated dozens or hundreds of units away.

Mitigating Z-Fighting with the Near Clipping Plane

Adjusting the camera frustum's near plane is the single most effective way to eliminate z-fighting without changing rendering architecture or depth buffer bit-depth.

The precision distribution across the frustum is governed heavily by the ratio \(\frac{f}{n}\). Reducing this ratio distributes depth precision more evenly across the scene. While lowering \(f\) helps, increasing \(n\) has an exponentially greater impact because precision drops sharply as \(n\) approaches zero.

Best Practices for Frustum Configuration

  1. Push the Near Plane Outward: Keep \(n\) as large as possible without clipping objects positioned close to the camera. Increasing \(n\) from \(0.01\) to \(1.0\) reclaims orders of magnitude of depth resolution across medium and long distances.
  2. Minimize the Far-to-Near Ratio: Aim to keep the \(\frac{f}{n}\) ratio under \(10^4\) or \(10^5\) when using a standard 24-bit integer depth buffer.
  3. Avoid Default Minimal Values: Setting \(n = 0.001\) to prevent clipping often ruins depth fidelity across the entire scene. Instead, set \(n\) dynamically based on scene geometry or player scale (e.g., \(0.1\) or \(0.5\) in real-world scale units).

Additional Mitigations

If increasing the near plane causes unwanted clipping of foreground objects, consider these complementary strategies:

  • glPolygonOffset: Adds a constant and slope-dependent bias to fragment depth values, allowing coplanar decals or overlays to render predictably in front of base geometry.
  • Floating-Point Depth Buffers: Using a 32-bit floating-point buffer (GL_DEPTH_COMPONENT32F) provides higher dynamic range than integer depth buffers.
  • Reversed-Z Buffering: Pairing a floating-point depth buffer with inverted clip planes (\(n\) mapped to \(1.0\) and \(f\) mapped to \(0.0\)) creates a nearly uniform precision distribution throughout the frustum, virtually eliminating distance-based z-fighting.
