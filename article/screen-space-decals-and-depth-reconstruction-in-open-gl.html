Screen-Space Decals and Depth Reconstruction in OpenGL

Screen-space decal projection is a rendering technique used to project localized details—such as bullet holes, footprints, blood splatters, or road markings—onto arbitrary scene geometry without modifying the underlying meshes. Instead of clipping and generating static decal geometry on the CPU, this method renders a geometric proxy (typically a bounding box) and samples the existing scene depth. By unprojecting the depth buffer in OpenGL, the fragment shader reconstructs the precise world-space position of underlying geometry, transforms that position into decal-relative space, and maps texture coordinates across uneven surfaces efficiently.

The Concept of Screen-Space Decals

Traditional decals require cutting and creating custom polygonal geometry that conforms to the target surfaces, a process that is CPU-intensive and fails on complex or dynamic meshes. Screen-space decals eliminate geometry processing entirely by deferring the projection to the fragment stage.

A simple 3D proxy volume, usually a unit cube, is positioned and scaled in the world where the decal should appear. When this volume is rendered, its fragments cover the area of the screen where the decal might interact with the scene. The fragment shader then queries what is physically located inside that volume by reading the pre-rendered scene depth.

Depth Buffer Reconstruction in OpenGL

To apply a texture to the underlying surface, the GPU must know the exact 3D coordinates of the geometry existing underneath the decal box's rasterized pixels. This is achieved through depth reconstruction:

  1. Screen-to-NDC Conversion: The fragment shader accesses the screen-space coordinates via gl_FragCoord.xy and divides them by the viewport resolution to generate a normalized UV coordinate (u, v) \in [0, 1].
  2. Depth Retrieval: The hardware depth buffer is sampled at (u, v) using a depth texture (sampler2D). In OpenGL, the sampled depth value \(z_{win}\) sits in the range \([0, 1]\).
  3. Normalized Device Coordinates (NDC): The coordinates are mapped to the standard OpenGL \([-1, 1]\) NDC space: \[x_{ndc} = u \times 2.0 - 1.0\] \[y_{ndc} = v \times 2.0 - 1.0\] \[z_{ndc} = z_{win} \times 2.0 - 1.0\]
  4. Unprojecting via the Inverse Matrix: The NDC vector \((x_{ndc}, y_{ndc}, z_{ndc}, 1.0)\) is multiplied by the inverse of the camera’s View-Projection matrix: \[\mathbf{P}_{clip} = (\mathbf{V} \cdot \mathbf{P})^{-1} \times \mathbf{P}_{ndc}\]
  5. Perspective Division: Dividing the resulting vector by its \(w\)-component produces the exact world-space position: \[\mathbf{P}_{world} = \mathbf{P}_{clip}.xyz / \mathbf{P}_{clip}.w\]

Mapping to Decal Space and Clamping

Once the world-space position \(\mathbf{P}_{world}\) is obtained, it is converted into the local coordinate space of the decal proxy by multiplying it by the inverse of the decal's model matrix:

\[\mathbf{P}_{local} = \mathbf{M}_{decal}^{-1} \times \mathbf{P}_{world}\]

If the decal proxy is a unit cube spanning \([-0.5, 0.5]\) on all axes:

  • The shader checks if \(\mathbf{P}_{local}\) falls outside the range \([-0.5, 0.5]\). If any component exceeds these bounds, the fragment is discarded using the discard keyword, ensuring the decal does not bleed outside its designated volume.
  • If the position is valid, two of the local coordinates (for instance, the \(X\) and \(Z\) or \(X\) and \(Y\) coordinates, depending on projection orientation) are shifted from \([-0.5, 0.5]\) to \([0, 1]\) by adding \(0.5\).
  • These mapped values serve as standard UV coordinates to sample the decal's diffuse, normal, and roughness textures.

Surface Normal Clipping and Blending

Projecting textures straight through a 3D box can cause severe texture stretching on surfaces that are parallel to the direction of projection. In a deferred rendering pipeline, the fragment shader reads the surface normal from the G-buffer; in a forward pipeline, the surface normal can be approximated using screen-space derivatives (dFdx and dFdy on the reconstructed world position).

By calculating the dot product between the surface normal and the decal's forward projection vector, the shader can detect steep angles and blend the decal's opacity to zero, preventing visual distortion across steep slopes and perpendicular walls.
