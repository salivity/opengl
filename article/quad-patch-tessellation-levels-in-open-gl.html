Quad Patch Tessellation Levels in OpenGL

In OpenGL, tessellation levels for a quad patch dictate how a four-sided parametric surface is subdivided into a grid of smaller geometric primitives. These levels are computed in the Tessellation Control Shader (TCS) and output via gl_TessLevelOuter[4] and gl_TessLevelInner[2]. While the OpenGL pipeline consumes these floating-point values to generate the domain topology, the developer mathematically defines these factors based on geometric, visual, and performance metrics such as camera distance, screen-space target size, surface curvature, and continuous Level of Detail (LOD) interpolation.

The Role of Outer and Inner Tessellation Levels

A quad patch domain is defined in normalized parametric coordinates \((u, v) \in [0, 1]^2\). The subdivision behavior is governed by two sets of values:

  • Outer Tessellation Levels (gl_TessLevelOuter): Four values that determine the number of segments along each perimeter edge.
    • gl_TessLevelOuter[0]: Subdivisions along the edge where \(u = 0\) (left edge).
    • gl_TessLevelOuter[1]: Subdivisions along the edge where \(v = 0\) (bottom edge).
    • gl_TessLevelOuter[2]: Subdivisions along the edge where \(u = 1\) (right edge).
    • gl_TessLevelOuter[3]: Subdivisions along the edge where \(v = 1\) (top edge).
  • Inner Tessellation Levels (gl_TessLevelInner): Two values that control the internal grid density.
    • gl_TessLevelInner[0]: Internal horizontal subdivisions, aligning with outer edges 1 and 3.
    • gl_TessLevelInner[1]: Internal vertical subdivisions, aligning with outer edges 0 and 2.

If any outer level evaluates to less than or equal to zero, the entire patch is culled by the primitive generator.

Mathematical Factors Determining Outer Levels

Outer levels must match across neighboring patches to prevent cracking and T-junctions along shared boundaries. Consequently, each outer level is typically computed purely as a function of the two vertices forming that specific boundary edge:

  1. Euclidean Distance to the Camera: The edge midpoint \(\mathbf{M} = \frac{\mathbf{P}_a + \mathbf{P}_b}{2}\) is measured against the camera position \(\mathbf{C}\): \[d = \|\mathbf{M} - \mathbf{C}\|\] The tessellation factor is calculated via an inverse or linear interpolation mapping: \[\text{Level} = \text{clamp}\left( \text{Level}_{\max} - \frac{d - d_{\min}}{d_{\max} - d_{\min}} \cdot (\text{Level}_{\max} - \text{Level}_{\min}),\, \text{Level}_{\min},\, \text{Level}_{\max} \right)\]

  2. Screen-Space Projected Edge Length: To maintain a uniform pixel density for triangles, the world-space length of an edge \(\|\mathbf{P}_b - \mathbf{P}_a\|\) is projected onto the view plane using camera focal length and perspective division: \[L_{\text{screen}} \approx \frac{\|\mathbf{P}_b - \mathbf{P}_a\| \cdot f}{d}\] \[\text{Level} = \frac{L_{\text{screen}}}{\text{TargetPixelSize}}\]

  3. Edge Curvature and Normal Variation: When displacement mapping or curved PN-triangles/quads are used, edges with significant deviation in vertex normals require higher sampling: \[\Delta \theta = \arccos(\mathbf{N}_a \cdot \mathbf{N}_b)\] \[\text{Level} = \text{BaseLevel} \cdot (1.0 + k \cdot \Delta \theta)\]

  4. Silhouette and View-Angle Sensitivity: Edges oriented perpendicular to the view vector (forming the silhouette of an object) require higher density to prevent visible polygonal outlines: \[S = 1.0 - |\mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{V}|\] where \(\mathbf{N}\) is the averaged edge normal and \(\mathbf{V}\) is the normalized view vector.

Mathematical Factors Determining Inner Levels

Inner levels govern the interior mesh density and are usually derived as functions of the outer levels to ensure a smooth transition from the boundary to the interior:

  1. Opposing Edge Averaging: To preserve proportional aspect ratios across the grid, inner levels are commonly calculated as the arithmetic or geometric mean of opposing boundary levels: \[\text{gl\_TessLevelInner}[0] = \frac{\text{gl\_TessLevelOuter}[1] + \text{gl\_TessLevelOuter}[3]}{2}\] \[\text{gl\_TessLevelInner}[1] = \frac{\text{gl\_TessLevelOuter}[0] + \text{gl\_TessLevelOuter}[2]}{2}\]

  2. Maximum Bound Preservation: To avoid undersampling when adjacent edges differ drastically in scale, the maximum function can be used: \[\text{gl\_TessLevelInner}[0] = \max(\text{gl\_TessLevelOuter}[1],\, \text{gl\_TessLevelOuter}[3])\] \[\text{gl\_TessLevelInner}[1] = \max(\text{gl\_TessLevelOuter}[0],\, \text{gl\_TessLevelOuter}[2])\]

  3. Patch Centroid Metrics: When interior displacement (such as heightmap peaks) exists inside the quad, the inner levels can be computed using the distance and roughness at the patch center \(\mathbf{P}_{\text{center}} = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i=0}^3 \mathbf{P}_i\), independent of the boundaries, and subsequently clamped to the maximum outer factor to prevent degenerate interior stretching.

Primitive Generation and Spacing Modes

The calculated floating-point values are clamped by hardware to the implementation-defined limit (queried with GL_MAX_TESS_GEN_LEVEL, typically 64). The final vertex distribution is resolved in the Tessellation Evaluation Shader (TES) based on the declared spacing mode:

  • equal_spacing: Values are rounded up to the nearest integer, and segments are partitioned equally.
  • fractional_even_spacing / fractional_odd_spacing: Values are clamped to the nearest even or odd integer, smoothly transitioning new geometry into existence to eliminate visible popping artifacts during camera motion.
